Derivation 1Galois conjugationhours

The single highest-value item, and it operates entirely inside the ℤ[φ] system set up in the Golden Arithmetic lab.

The map. φ was built from √5. There is a second, equally valid square root: −√5. Swapping them is a field automorphism — call it σ. It leaves every ordinary rational number alone and moves only the √5 part.

φ  = (1 + √5)/2
σ(φ) = (1 − √5)/2 = 1 − φ

check:  1 − 1.618034 = −0.618034 = −1/φ   ✓

σ sends φ to the other root. That is the whole content.

On a general element

σ(a + bφ) = a + b(1 − φ) = (a + b) − bφ

Why this is the load-bearing derivation

Fusion coefficients Nabc are integers. σ fixes every integer. Therefore σ leaves the entire fusion ring untouched — one line, no machinery.

But σ sends dτ = φ to −1/φ. Same ring, different quantum dimension. That is exactly Fibonacci → Yang–Lee, and it is why Rowell–Stong–Wang can write "the Galois conjugate of the Fibonacci theory is the Yang-Lee theory" as a plain statement of fact.

"One modulus, two arguments" has a mechanism, and this is it.

Derivation 2The modular group, and why S existsa day

⚠ Notation collision — read before anything else

τ means two entirely different things in this literature. The Fibonacci anyon is τ. The modular parameter of a torus is also τ. They are unrelated and appear in the same papers. Watch the context; there is no fixing it.

A torus has two independent loops — call them a (around the hole) and b (through it). Nothing distinguishes them intrinsically. So there must be an operation that swaps them, and that operation is S. A second operation shears one along the other: T. Together they generate SL(2,ℤ).

On the modular parameter:

S :  τ → −1/τ        swap the two cycles
T :  τ → τ + 1       shear

What does the S-matrix physically represent?

In S² = I, why do the off-diagonal entries vanish?

S² = (1/𝒟²) ⎡ 1·1 + φ·φ    1·φ + φ·(−1) ⎤
            ⎣ φ·1 + (−1)·φ  φ·φ + (−1)(−1) ⎦

   = (1/𝒟²) ⎡ 1 + φ²     0     ⎤
            ⎣    0    φ² + 1   ⎦

and  1 + φ² = 𝒟²   so

S² = ⎡ 1  0 ⎤   =  the identity
     ⎣ 0  1 ⎦

The off-diagonal entries cancel before you divide by anything: φ − φ = 0. The diagonal ones are 𝒟²/𝒟². Nothing was approximated.

What you have just shown

Swapping the torus's two cycles twice returns you to where you started — and the anyon S-matrix knows this. S is not an arbitrary array of numbers; it is a geometric operation on a doughnut, written down. That is why tutorial 4 opens with a torus.

Derivation 4Lee–Yang zeros, on two sitesa day

The circle theorem is usually presented as a deep result. On two spins you can derive it in ten lines and see exactly why a transition is zeros pinching the real axis.

⚠ Read the case of the letters

Z (capital) is the partition function. z (lowercase) is the fugacity, e2βh. They are different objects and they appear in the same equation. Statistical mechanics does this constantly — it is the same trap as τ meaning both the anyon and the torus modulus. Writing them visibly differently on paper is a legitimate defence.

Two Ising spins in a field: E = −J s₁s₂ − h(s₁+s₂), each s = ±1. Four states.

↑↑ :  E = −J − 2h
↑↓ :  E = +J
↓↑ :  E = +J
↓↓ :  E = −J + 2h

Z = eβ(J+2h) + 2e−βJ + eβ(J−2h)

write  K = eβJ,  z = e2βh

Z = K z + 2/K + K/z

You have just proved the Lee–Yang circle theorem for N = 2

|z| = 1 exactly. The zeros of the partition function lie on the unit circle, for every temperature. That is the theorem, and on two sites it is four lines of algebra.

And now the part that teaches the physics

Track where on the circle the zeros sit as temperature changes:

K = eβJtemperaturezangle from +real axis

Physical magnetic fields are real, which means z sits on the positive real axis — angle 0. A phase transition happens only if a zero reaches it.

The punchline, and it is a real result

As T → 0 the zeros march only as far as ±90°. They never reach the positive real axis. Therefore the 1D Ising model has no phase transition at any finite temperature — which is correct, and famous, and you just derived it by watching two complex numbers fail to arrive.

In 2D they do pinch. That is the whole difference.

The canonical calculable example, and the right place to start: a spin-½ in a magnetic field, with the field direction traversing a closed loop on the sphere. The Berry phase comes out as

γ = −½ Ω        Ω = solid angle enclosed by the path

Do that one first. It is in every graduate text, it is fully computable, and it establishes the shape: a phase that depends only on the geometry of the path, not on how fast you traverse it.
