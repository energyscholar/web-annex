Tutorial 1 · fusion rings, modular categories, and the edge

One Modulus,
Two Arguments

Two different physical theories share the Fibonacci fusion rule. One is a universal quantum computer. The other is a critical point. The fusion algebra cannot tell them apart — and that is not a defect, it is the content.

What this is

A worksheet, not a result. The mathematics on this page is standard topological quantum computation, roughly 1984–2009. None of it is new. “It was simply new to my hands. I had read it for years and never computed with it, and those turn out to be different states of knowing.” These sheets exist to move a reader from the first state to the second, and they make no claim of their own.

The method is predict–then–check. Every input box, drill and gate here is built for one move: write down what you think the number is before you reveal it. Numbers are sourced to a paper where a paper says them, and marked unchecked where none does — a sheet whose whole method is check it yourself cannot ask you to take its own arithmetic on trust.

If you are coming from an operator formalism, you may want to ask of every quantity here what the declared observable is, and what mapping puts it into the domain before the algebra acts. That is a fair question, and these sheets sit upstream of it — the arithmetic of d² = d + 1 is the same arithmetic whichever way it is answered. They take no position on it. The full note is on the index.

Prerequisites: the Golden Arithmetic lab (φ, and the identity φ² = φ + 1). Derivation 1 (Galois conjugation) opens Section 3 and Derivation 3 (the central charge) opens Section 6 — both are in the paper course. No Berry phase anywhere in this one.

Start from a number. The rule τ ⊗ τ = 1 ⊕ τ gives the matrix [[0, 1], [1, 1]], whose largest eigenvalue is φ = 1.618… — the same φ the Golden Arithmetic lab reduces by hand. This tutorial is about exactly one thing: what that single number does, and does not, tell you about the theory it came out of. The answer is that four different theories hand you the same φ, and separating them takes data the matrix does not contain.

Section 1A fusion ring is just a matrix of non-negative integers

Start with the thing that is not in dispute. Anyon types combine, and the rule for combining them is bookkeeping: if you bring type a next to type b, what can you get?

The Fibonacci rule has two objects — the vacuum 1 and one nontrivial anyon τ — and exactly one interesting line.

Write that as a matrix whose entries count the ways each outcome can occur:

Nτ = [[0, 1], [1, 1]]Rows and columns indexed by (1, τ). Every entry is a non-negative integer — it counts channels.

This matrix is the fusion ring. It is combinatorics. It contains no phases, no complex numbers, no notion of unitarity, and no physics beyond "what can fuse to what."

The modulus falls out immediately

A non-negative integer matrix has a largest real eigenvalue — its Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue. Apply Nτ over and over and watch what the ratio does.

Section 2Two roots, one polynomial

A fusion ring is not yet a physical theory. To get one you must assign each object an actual quantum dimension da, behaving multiplicatively under fusion. Consistency forces dτ to satisfy the same quadratic the matrix does — and a quadratic has two roots.

The refinement worth taking away

Perron–Frobenius dimension and quantum dimension are two different things, and unitarity is precisely the condition that they agree. The PF dimension is φ in both theories — a property of the ring. The quantum dimension is φ or −1/φ — a property of the category. The slogan "you cannot derive an argument from a modulus" is sharper than it first sounds: the modulus does not even fix the dimension, only the polynomial the dimension solves.

✓ The claim survives, and gets a better name. Rowell–Stong–Wang say it in their own introduction: "the Galois conjugate of the Fibonacci theory is the Yang-Lee theory, which is non-unitary." Galois action provably fixes the fusion matrices — a named group action that holds the ring rigid while moving everything else. That is "one modulus, two arguments" as a theorem rather than an observation. The two dimensions are the roots of RSW's own Theorem 3.1 at m = 1.

Section 4What Rowell–Stong–Wang actually does

The paper is Rowell, Stong & Wang, "On classification of modular tensor categories" (Comm. Math. Phys. 292, 2009; arXiv:0712.1377). It gives a complete classification of MTCs of low rank — rank meaning the number of anyon types.

The method is what matters. Rather than constructing categories and hoping to find them all, they turn the consistency conditions into Diophantine constraints and solve them. Number theory does the classifying, and the output is a finite explicit list at each low rank together with a proof that nothing else exists.

Admissible quantum dimensions below 2 are exactly 2cos(π/n): 1, √2, φ, √3, 1.8019, … There is nothing between √2 and φ. The spectrum is discrete, so the gap is forbidden by representation theory — which is precisely the Gell-Mann situation, where the classification itself forbids the intermediate case.a note made before reading Rowell–Stong–Wang

That instinct was right, and RSW is its stronger form: the Jones index theorem constrains the dimensions, RSW classifies the categories. Both make an empty slot rigorous instead of rhetorical.

⚠⚠ The trap in citing RSW — read this before quoting a number from it

RSW classify UNITARY modular tensor categories. Yang–Lee fails their Frobenius–Perron criterion and does not appear in their tables at all.

So RSW cannot be cited for a Yang–Lee value, and it cannot be cited for the count of four categories over this fusion ring — that count includes non-unitary members its classification excludes by construction. Reaching into a table for the number you want, when the table was built to leave that case out, is a citation error and not a shortcut.

What RSW does give you here is the Galois statement, quoted above, which is in the introduction and is about the pair rather than about either member's twist.

A hard constraint that changes what to look for

Freedman's Galois Conjugates of Topological Phases, Theorem IV.5, is a proof that no gapped Hermitian Lieb–Robinson Hamiltonian has a Yang–Lee phase as its ground state — in any gauge. Sanno 2022 cites it and routes around it with an open dissipative system.

Non-unitary is not merely unusual here. It is excluded from gapped Hermitian ground states by theorem. If one hoped to find a naturally occurring Yang–Lee medium, the obvious route is closed, and the open-system route is the one Sanno had to take.

Section 5Reidemeister isn't one thing, and the split is the physics

Knot theory treats the Reidemeister moves as the invariance group — what survives a wiggle. That is right for knots. It is not right for anyons, because anyon worldlines are not curves but ribbons, carrying a framing.

The point

R1's failure is not a defect in the classification. It is an observable. The amount by which R1 fails is the topological spin — the same θτ that distinguishes the four cells of Fig. 4.

R2/R3-invariance gives the braid representation, the phase. The fusion matrix's PF eigenvalue gives φ, the magnitude. R1 is the move that couples them.

Section 6The Gauss sum is the weld

Magnitude and phase do not stay separate. In any modular tensor category one identity ties them together:

Σa da² θa = 𝒟 · e2πi c−/8𝒟 = total quantum dimension. Sum over all anyon types.

Dimensions weighted by twists on the left; a single number — the chiral central charge, a criticality quantity living on the gapless boundary — on the right. For Fibonacci the sum has just two terms, and you can watch them land.

So the bulk's braiding data determines the boundary's critical theory. "The edge between topology and criticality" reads literally.
