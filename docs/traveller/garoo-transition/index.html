The Unmasking of Garoo

For nine decades the Health Directorate governed a world on the principle that sickness was a choice, and therefore a crime. It did not fall to an invasion, a famine, or a war. It fell because a great many of the wrong people got a rash.

By Dr. Halle Ostrom, Investigative Fellow · Institute of Polity Studies, University of Mora
Filed from Garoo Highport (0130) · Third revision, cleared for circulation

The Autarchy of Garoo was, until quite recently, the most orderly polity in this arm of the Marches. It achieved this by a simple instrument. Illness was legally defined as a failure of personal discipline; visible illness was a public failure, and therefore an offence. The Health Police — properly the Directorate of Public Vitality, though nobody called it that — enforced the definition with ceremonial scalpels, sonic disruptors, and an absolute discretion that no court reviewed.

I have spent four months on and above this world assembling an account of how that ended. I will say at the outset that I have not established a cause for the event that began it, and that I do not find the official explanation credible. What follows is what can be documented.

An affliction with no vector

In the early part of last year, a dermatological condition appeared in the upper residential terraces of Garoo's three arcologies. It was not serious. It was, by every clinical account I have obtained, cosmetic — an eruption of inflamed pustular lesions across the face, neck and hands, persisting six to eleven weeks and resolving without treatment or scarring.

Three features of it are worth the reader's attention.

The first is that it was not infectious. No vector was ever established. It did not spread to the service levels, the manufactories, or the refugee terraces, though those populations share air, water and transit with the households that were affected. Whatever it was, it did not move from person to person.

The second is that onset was simultaneous. Not sequential, as an outbreak would be — simultaneous, across households with no contact, in a window of about nine days. One clinician I interviewed, who asked not to be named and who is now serving on the provisional council's medical board, described it to me as "less like a contagion than like a clock going off."

The third is that it was confined by wealth. The affected were, almost without exception, the households of Directorate officers, senior arcology administrators, the mercantile families, and the Autarch's own extended court — the several thousand people on Garoo who could afford imported personal goods.

The Directorate's own inquiry considered and dismissed a number of theories, including a consignment of offworld cosmetic preparations that had cleared customs some weeks earlier. The inquiry's file records that this line was "closed as unproductive." I have requested the underlying customs manifests four times and been refused four times, most recently by an office that no longer exists.

The law had one sentence for a visible eruption: it was a crime against the state. The law did not have a second sentence for what to do when the state was the one erupting.

The law that was never for them

Here is what the Directorate did, faced with several thousand visibly ill members of the governing class.

Nothing.

No detentions were recorded in the upper terraces during the affected period. No sanitary certificates were revoked. No households were placed under the vitality restrictions that had, in the same calendar year, been applied to eleven thousand people in the lower districts for conditions substantially less visible — in several documented instances, for eczema, for shaving rash, and in one case which I have confirmed from three sources, for freckles.

The Directorate did not announce an exemption. It did not amend the statute. It simply did not enforce, and it did not explain why not.

I want to be precise about the scale of what this revealed, because it is easy to state and hard to feel. Garoo's population had been taught for three generations that illness was moral failure and that enforcement was mercy — that the scalpel was applied to the sick for the sake of the well. Then the entire apparatus of that teaching stood in public with its face visibly broken out, and arrested nobody.

The masking

Under the vitality statutes, face coverings were prohibited to the general population. Concealment of the face was concealment of one's condition and was itself an offence. The single exception was the Directorate's own senior officers, whose masks were an emblem of rank and were worn precisely because they did not have to justify themselves.

So when the governing class wanted to hide their faces, they had exactly two options: break the law, or claim the privilege.

Many claimed the privilege. Masks appeared on people who had never held Directorate rank — on merchants, on administrators, on the Autarch's cousins — and the Health Police, who arrested a woman in Arco-3 the same month for a covered cheek, said nothing.

What happened next was not organised, and I have found no evidence that anyone planned it. On the ninth of the tenth month, the shift crews leaving the Arco-2 manufactories came out masked. Not all of them; perhaps a third. They were not carrying anything and they did not chant. They walked home.

The Directorate arrested one hundred and forty of them. The following shift came out masked in its entirety.

"You have to understand what it meant. For ninety years they told us that covering your face was a lie you told about your own body. And then they covered theirs. So we thought — all right. We'll lie too. Let's all lie together and see who has the nerve to say it."
— Arco-2 lift technician, interviewed under condition of anonymity

The police who stopped policing

The institution then came apart along a fault that had always been in it. The Health Police were recruited overwhelmingly from the lower terraces — the same districts supplying the masked shifts — and officered from the upper. Junior enforcement personnel began declining to execute detention orders. This was not, initially, a matter of principle; several officers I interviewed described it as arithmetic. There was nowhere to put anyone.

The single episode that everyone on this world can date precisely occurred at the Arco-1 transit concourse, where a Directorate line was ordered to clear a masked crowd of several thousand with sonic disruptors. The order was given three times. It was not carried out. The senior officer present — masked, and by every account visibly afflicted himself — put up his weapon and walked off the concourse, and his line went with him.

There was violence afterwards, and I will not minimise it. Directorate stations were burned in all three arcologies. At least four hundred people died in the following six weeks, the great majority of them Health Police caught individually and killed by crowds. A number of them were the junior personnel who had refused their orders. The distinction between the officers who had administered the statutes and the constables who had merely enforced them was not one the crowds were interested in making, and I have not met anyone here who defends what was done, or anyone who is surprised by it.

The Autarch's court dispersed over roughly a fortnight. The Directorate was formally dissolved by an instrument issued in the Autarch's name, though whether it was issued by the Autarch is one of the several things about that period nobody will discuss with an offworlder.

Reconstructed chronology

Weeks 1–3 · the consignment
Imported goods clear Garoo customs. Unremarkable at the time; noted here only because the Directorate's inquiry later considered and dismissed it.
Weeks 7–9 · onset
Simultaneous appearance of the condition across unconnected upper-terrace households. No vector established. No spread beyond the affected class.
Months 2–4 · the silence
No enforcement in the upper terraces. Enforcement continues, and rises, in the lower districts. No exemption announced.
Month 4 · the privilege
Directorate masks appear on persons holding no Directorate rank.
Month 5, day 9 · the first masked shift
Arco-2 manufactory crews leave work masked. 140 detained. The next shift masks entirely.
Months 5–6 · capacity failure
19,000+ detained against 4,000 places. Releases within hours. Junior enforcement personnel begin declining orders.
Month 6 · the Arco-1 concourse
A dispersal order refused three times. The line withdraws.
Months 6–8 · collapse and reprisal
Stations burned in all three arcologies. ~400 dead, mostly Health Police. Court disperses. Directorate formally dissolved.

What is there now

A provisional council sits in Arco-1. It has forty-one members and no agreed method of choosing the forty-second. Its authority is accepted in the arcologies, contested in the agricultural belt, and simply absent in the orbital and outsystem holdings, where the Summersun Mercenary Co-operative continues to run the starport very much as it did before, on the reasonable grounds that nobody has told it to stop and the traffic still needs handling.

The vitality statutes are suspended, not repealed. The medical infrastructure they justified — which was, it should be said, genuinely excellent — is intact and substantially unstaffed, because a great many of the people who ran it have left, been killed, or are declining to identify themselves as having run it.

Three factions are worth naming. The council's dominant bloc are lower-terrace organisers who want the statutes repealed outright. A second bloc, drawn from the surviving administrative class, wants them retained in amended form, and argues — not without evidence — that a world of this density with this atmosphere will have an epidemic within the decade without them. The third are the remnants of the Directorate, who have not disappeared, who retain their training and a good deal of their equipment, and who are currently doing nothing at all in a manner that a number of people here find more alarming than if they were doing something.

Offworld traffic has increased sharply. Garoo has spent ninety years as a place that ships avoided where they could, and it is now a place with a starport, no functioning customs regime, and an acute shortage of almost everything. What is arriving is not, in the main, humanitarian.

Garoo is not dangerous in the way it was. It is dangerous in a new way, and nobody here — including me — can tell you yet what shape that will take.

The agitators who were not there

Throughout the affected period the Directorate's public position was that the condition had been introduced deliberately by offworld agitators. This was the Directorate's public position about a great many things, including several documented instances in which the accused could be shown to have been elsewhere, or not to exist.

A persistent strand of opinion on this world — which I met in every arcology, and which I do not share — holds that the agitators were specific, and named. The claim is that Garoo was brought down by the offworld crew known from the popular broadcasts as the Heroes of Raschev.

I want to deal with this properly, because it is widely believed and because it is nonsense.

The entire evidentiary basis for it is a single recording. In the final year of the Autarchy the Directorate circulated a broadcast which purported to show one of that crew — the Aslan, Ora — in the act of assassinating the Autarch. It was played continuously for some weeks. A warrant followed. On this world it is still, in some quarters, treated as settled fact.

The recording is a fabrication. This is not my finding. It is the finding of the Directorate's own forensic section, whose internal assessment I have read in the recovered Arco-2 files: the sequence is synthetic, the assessment is unambiguous, and it is dated eleven days after the broadcast first aired. The assessment was never published. The broadcast was never withdrawn. The warrant was never cancelled, and I understand at least one such warrant remains outstanding against a party the provisional council cannot locate and appears disinclined to look for.

It is characteristic of this period that the state manufactured proof of a crime, was privately told within a fortnight that its proof was manufactured, and went on broadcasting it anyway. When that assessment surfaced during the collapse — and it surfaced early, because the forensic section was one of the first to stop taking orders — it did the Directorate more damage than the original accusation had ever done to the accused. People who had believed the recording did not conclude that they had been mistaken. They concluded that they had been managed, and that they had probably been managed about everything else.

So the offworld-agitator theory can be dismissed, and I dismiss it. Those who still hold it are reasoning backwards from a video they have been told is false and have decided not to believe. A fabricated proof is not evidence of anything except the character of whoever fabricated it.

I would add only this, and then leave it. The fabrication was thorough, it was official, and it was aimed at exactly the question a historian would most like answered. Whether any offworlder had any hand in what happened on this world is now a question that cannot be settled either way, by me or by anyone, because the only evidence ever offered for it was invented by a government with every reason to invent it. That is not the same as an answer of no. It is the permanent absence of an answer, which the Directorate purchased for itself and, in the end, could not spend.

A note on method and its limits. This account rests on 61 interviews, the Directorate's own detention logs for the relevant period (recovered from the Arco-2 station and partially fire-damaged), the forensic section's internal assessments, provisional council minutes, and Highport traffic records. It does not rest on customs data, which I was unable to obtain. The origin of the condition remains unestablished. I am aware of four theories in circulation on this world and I find none of them supported. I would caution readers against the assumption that a small cause cannot have a large effect; the historical literature on this point is not encouraging, and a polity which has staked everything on a single claim about the world is a polity with a single point of failure. What that failure will be is not usually predictable in advance, and is often, in retrospect, embarrassing.
