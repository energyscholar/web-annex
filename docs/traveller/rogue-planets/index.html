Rogue Worlds of the Rift

A Deep-Space Survey Dossier · District 268

Compiled from long-range astronomy & the naval survey of record · for crew review prior to transit

Two worlds drift in the empty gap between this frontier's settled clusters — no star, no daylight, only the dark. Both are candidates for the one thing this expedition needs: a place to refuel across a rift an ordinary ship cannot otherwise cross. This dossier is everything the surveys and the record can tell you before you arrive. What the surveys could not see, you will fill in yourselves.

Section IThe Rift & the Rogues

District 268 rift — two clusters of settled stars, an empty gap between, and the rogues that drift in it.

Rogue planets — worlds ejected from their birth systems to wander starless — were once thought rare. They are not. Current estimates put them at six to twenty times more numerous than starsOld-Terra microlensing surveys (MOA, OGLE) and direct imaging (PSO J318.5-22, WISE 0855) established that free-floating planets vastly outnumber the stars — on the order of one per cubic parsec. Most are small, cold, and all but undetectable. — the galaxy may hold trillions of them. This rift alone hides dozens. Most are dark and beneath notice. Two are not.

Backtrack their motion across the sky and both Arabus and Nyrthus trace to a single origin: the star Walston, which flung them out together roughly 1.5 million years ago. How we knowA rogue's proper motion — its drift against the background stars — runs backward like a clock. Two objects whose tracks converge on one star, at one moment, are siblings from one event. The same method traces runaway stars and interstellar visitors. They are siblings, thrown out in one event as their crowded birth system tore itself apartA young system that forms too many giant planets is unstable: over time the crowd scatters, slinging its members out one by one. Something more massive than Arabus did the throwing — Walston likely still carries a distant, unseen companion..

The rift is why any of this matters. Arabus sits squarely in it — a single stepping-stone that lets an ordinary drive cross the gap. Nyrthus lies deeper on the far side, useful only to a longer-legged ship.

Section IIArabus — the Ember

Arabus, its moon Typhon and the region of "unusual EM activity," the iceteroid belt, and the horizon beyond which a maneuver drive fails.

Arabus is almost a star. Eight times Jupiter's mass, a hair below the line where fusion ignites, it glows on its own — cloud-tops near 1,100 K, lit not by any sun but by the slow heat of its own contraction. A sub-brown-dwarfBelow ~13 Jupiter masses an object never fuses hydrogen, but a massive one still radiates the leftover heat of its formation for a long time — a self-luminous "failed star," cherry-red in the deep infrared. It carries a fierce magnetosphereA planetary magnetic field can host a whole ecology of plasma and current — witness the Io torus at Jupiter. Old-Terra thinkers speculated openly that such environments might even organize into something lifelike (Sagan & Salpeter, 1976; Tsytovich et al., 2007, on self-organizing plasma structures). The unity of critical-transition mathematics that would allow it was first mapped in the early Terran period — cf. B. Stephenson et al., c. 2026. and a volcanic moon, Typhon.

Do not skim it for fuel. Escape velocity at the cloud-tops is near 170 km/s — a skim run is a suicide profile for any hull. Its fuel, if any, lies in the ring of iceteroids that orbits it: leftover debris from its own formation, deep-frozen out where the ember's heat is nothing.

One caution the record insists on. Eleven years ago a scout under Lt. Sandoval jumped here to survey Arabus and never returned. Her drop tanks were recovered at her departure point — proof she set outDrop tanks are jettisoned at the departure point as a ship enters jump, not at the destination. Finding them at her origin confirms she fuelled up and jumped toward Arabus. — but at Arabus itself the search found nothing: no wreck, no beacon, only "unusual electromagnetic activity." The Navy logged it presumed misjump and did not look closer. Perhaps they should have.A vanished ship, no wreckage, and a magnetosphere humming with organized EM. Draw your own conclusions — the surveys drew none.

Section IIINyrthus — the Ice World

Nyrthus: a frozen super-Earth. Beneath a 4,280 km ice shell, the surveys infer a liquid ocean kept warm from within.

Nyrthus is a super-Earth wrapped in four thousand kilometres of ice. Beneath that shell the surveys infer an ocean of liquid water — kept from freezing not by sunlight but by the heat of radioactive decayUranium, thorium and potassium in a rocky world's interior release heat for billions of years. Under a thick, insulating ice cap a starless planet can hold a subsurface sea indefinitely — the mechanism proposed for interstellar worlds (Stevenson, 1999), and the same principle that keeps Europa's and Enceladus's oceans liquid. in its rocky heart, trapped under the insulating ice. Being a super-Earth only helps: more mass means more radioactives, and heat that lingers longer.

For your purposes Nyrthus is the easy answer: its surface ice is fuel you can reach — melt it, split it, done. If Arabus turns you away, Nyrthus is the fallback.

Section IVThe Fuel Problem

The catch at Arabus is physics. A maneuver drive is graviticA maneuver drive works against the gravity of a nearby mass — it grips the curvature of space around a body. Far from any large mass, in true deep space, its efficiency collapses and a ship can barely move. — it pushes against the gravity of a nearby mass. Out in the belt, far from Arabus, there is little to push against; a ship there is all but adrift, unable to cruise from one rock to the next.

And a jump can only bring you to within a hundred diametersJump too near a mass and the field collapses. For a huge body like Arabus that's a wide exclusion (~0.1 AU); for a small iceteroid it's only ~40,000 km — close enough to arrive right at its doorstep. of a body. For a small iceteroid that is close enough to arrive on its very doorstep — but only if you know exactly where the doorstep is: a five-hundred-kilometre rock, moving, in the dark. Find the right one, know its precise track, and the belt becomes an endless fuel depot. Without it, you jump into the black and reach nothing.

That is the shape of the problem this crew has to solve.
