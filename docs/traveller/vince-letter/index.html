All well. The rains came late but they came. The new dormitory block is roofed and Signe has opinions about the kitchen which she expresses daily.

He gets a look when he talks about it. Vince listens to him more than he lets on.

— M.A.
M. Aliyev · compound report≈4½ months old

A commercial matter, which I mention because my uncle is in a temper about it and you will hear about it eventually anyway.

Garoo has paid for this quarter's shipments in a new currency. Not the old one — the old one is not money any more. The new one is issued by whatever is governing them now. Three brokers in Loka have refused it outright and the fourth accepted it and is regretting it publicly.

Vince's view is that Garoo will sort itself out, as it always has, and that we should keep loading the ships and settle the accounts later — which is, to be fair to him, exactly what we have always done. Councillor Fost's view is that we are giving away a harvest for paper. This is the first time I have ever thought Fost had a point, which I have not said to anyone but you.

— M.A.

Part 2 of 3 — Vince Aliyev · Raschev · sent at his own cost

Friends,

I have started this letter four times. I will send this one whatever it looks like, because the courier leaves tonight and the next is in eleven days.

There are Garoovian warships over Raschev.

Four that we can see from the observatory at Loka, and their commander says there are others we cannot. I have no way to check that. We have telescopes and weather satellites. That is what we have.

They arrived without announcement and took station where they could be seen from the ground, which I am told is deliberate. Their commander — she gave a name, I will not put it in a letter — read me a statement. It was not a negotiation. She said the food ships would be loaded as they have always been loaded, on the schedule they have always kept, and that payment would be made in the new currency of the Provisional Authority of Garoo.

I told her that no bank on this world will take that currency, that no one here knows what it is worth, and that our people cannot eat a promise. She said she understood, and repeated the statement. I believe she did understand. I do not think she was permitted to say anything else.

Then she named Power One.

You will remember it — the fusion station on the rock off the northwest coast, that the Imperials built for us and then stopped paying for. It lights Loka and most of the peninsula. She did not threaten our cities and she did not threaten our people. She named the power station, and she said she hoped it would not be necessary, and I believe that too. It is a very careful sort of threat. It is the threat of a person who does not want to be a monster and has been given no other instrument.

We have loaded two convoys. I authorised it, and I want to be clear with you about what that did and did not cost, because the offworld version of this will be wrong.

We were going to send that food anyway. We have sent Garoo our surplus every season for three hundred years. There is nowhere else for it to go and no one else who wants it. Loading those ships was not a sacrifice and no one on this world went hungry. The problem is that nobody has paid us.

Their commander has promised payment. She has offered paper — receipts, drafts, an instrument she called a bond of the Provisional Authority — and some goods in trade, which were not nothing but were not much. I do not think she is lying. I think she has been sent here without money and told not to come back without the cargo.

We can carry this about a year. I have run the figures with the district factors and that is the honest number. A year of harvests handed over against paper. After that the farmers will stop planting the surplus, because a man who is not paid twice will not plant a third time, and no speech of mine will change his arithmetic. So this is not a famine and it is not next month. It is a debt with a fuse on it, and the fuse is about a year long.

What it has cost me is every argument I have with Councillor Fost from now until I die. He says, in public, that I cannot protect this world. He is right. That is what makes it hard to answer him.

Now I will tell you what I do not understand, in case you can make sense of it where I cannot.

Garoo is not starving. I have seen their agricultural returns for thirty years; they grow more food in those fungus galleries than we do in our whole southern belt. What we send them is not their bread. It is their delicacies. It is what their rich people used to eat on a holiday. And they have brought warships to take it.

I have read what the offworld journals say about their revolution. Some hundreds dead, a police force dissolved, a council of forty-one that cannot agree on the time of day. It reads like a bad month in a small country. It does not read like a reason to point guns at farmers over preserved fruit. Something is being left out and I do not have the education to work out what.

There is one more thing, and I put it here only because I am tired and it has been on my mind for thirty years.

Garoo has never sold us a starship. Not once, not at any price, and I have asked three times in my life. They build them — that great port of theirs turns out hulls for half the subsector — and they have always found a reason. When I was younger I thought it was money. It is not money. They have simply never wanted this world to have a ship of its own, and now four of theirs are sitting over my head and I understand the policy rather better than I did.

We could afford one now, I think. Not easily, and not without an argument that would last a year. But we are not the world we were when I last asked, and I find I mind the refusal more than I used to. That is not a proposal. It is just a thing I have been turning over, and there is no one here to say it to.

So I am asking. I have never asked you for anything.

Bring warships. Bring whatever you have and whatever you can borrow. You have friends in the Imperial service and I do not. You have a ship that fights and this world has never had one and never expected to need one. If four of their vessels are all there is, then perhaps one of yours standing between them and the harbour is enough to make somebody in that fleet ask a question they have not been asked.

And if I am wrong about that, come anyway, and tell me what to do instead. I would rather be corrected by you than be right on my own.

Your land is safe. Your compound is untouched — they have not come near the island and I have not drawn their attention to it. Thomas and Bob are well. The Aslan pair keep watch on the approaches and will not be talked out of it.

I know what I am asking, and roughly what it costs. Come if you can.

Vince Aliyev
Raschev
Part 3 of 3 — appended · not shown to the sender

He will not write down the part that matters, so I will.

When Garoo wanted you, they came to him. Not politely. They had names, they had the warrant, and they had a great deal to offer a small world. He sat in that room for two hours and told them he had never heard of you.

He has never once mentioned it to you and he would be furious that I have. He does not think of it as something you owe him. He thinks of it as something he did.

I am telling you because I want you to understand what it cost him to write I am asking, and because if you cannot come, he will need to hear that from you and not from silence.

Now the part that is mine and not his, and I have thought about whether to write it for a week.

Their commander asked to see me alone. Not my uncle. Me. She sent a junior officer to the compound gate with a written request, which I think she did so that it could be refused without anyone losing anything.

We sat in the port office for an hour and she did not read me a statement. She asked me what Raschev wants. Not what we would settle for — she was quite particular about the difference. What we want. She asked me twice, as though she expected me to have an answer ready and was disappointed that I did not.

Then she told me, more or less directly, that she has nothing to give us. No money, no goods worth the name, and — this is the part I keep turning over — no authority to agree to anything that is not on her list. She said she has sent three requests home for instructions and had two acknowledgements and no instructions.

So she is not what the statement made her sound like. She is a woman who has been sent a very long way with guns and no purse, and told not to come back with a refusal. She is bargaining because she has to bring something home, and she does not know what to bargain with, and she was asking a farmer's niece because there was nobody else to ask.

I do not know what to do with that. But I know it is worth more than the four ships, and I am not going to put it in a letter my uncle reads, because he would go and shout at her, and she is the only person up there who has asked us a question.

— Marta
