Critical Lines and Multicritical Points: the Models

Eight model systems, chosen because each one gives an instrument a known answer. The instrument is the Criticality Compass: its Detector classifies a system's critical geometry from observations, and its Navigator finds a critical line and moves along it. Every section shows a headline, one live graphic and a cyan “For the Compass” box with its accordions closed; open “Working equations” for the physicist's layer and “Full derivation” inside it for the deepest.

POINT: an isolated fixed point. The renormalisation-group flow stops at one place and has one escape direction among the symmetric couplings (2D Ising at zero field).
LINE: a continuum of fixed points. The flow stops wherever it lands; one direction is exactly marginal (Ashkin–Teller, XXZ).
WALKING: the flow slows to a small nonzero minimum, never stops, then leaves. Finite systems imitate criticality (Potts q = 5, 6).
REFUSE: the honest verdict when the data cannot tell. It is a cell of the table, the default one.

The organising idea is follow the flow: the Detector reads the velocity, accumulation and circulation of the flow over declared scale and control relations. In plain words: the velocity is how much a measured quantity changes from one scale (or one control value) to the next; the accumulation is that change added up along a chain of scales; the circulation is what is left over when you go round a small loop of scale and control steps; and a declared relation is the list of which measurements count as neighbours (this scale and the next, this control value and the next), fixed before the data are looked at. The techniques page teaches the machinery. 21 Quizzes are folded under each section; the final test at the end is drawn from a bank, so a retake gets different items. 0 Orientation: eight models on one alphabet

Each model is here because it gives the Compass a known answer: a point, a line or a walk, plus one case the alphabet has no letter for yet, a point with two relevant directions (multicritical).

The eight: Ashkin–Teller, Baxter’s eight-vertex model, two Ising layers coupled bilinearly, O(n1)⊕O(n2), Blume–Capel, the Potts model at q = 5 and 6, the XXZ chain with its six-vertex twin, and the self-organising adaptive networks of Sormunen, Gross and Saramäki. Click a miniature to jump to its section. Two of them are the gentlest on-ramps if the topic is new: Blume–Capel (spins that may be missing) and XXZ (a quantum spin chain whose anisotropy you dial). The Ashkin–Teller and coupled-Ising sections are a pair: the same two ingredients, coupled two ways, give two different geometries. The gallery near the end is the one-table summary.

1 Ashkin–Teller: a whole line of critical points

Two Ising layers joined by a four-spin coupling: instead of one critical point there is a whole line of them, and the exponents change continuously as you slide along it.

Every site carries two Ising spins, σ and τ. The coupling K rewards neighbouring σ's that agree, and neighbouring τ's that agree. The four-spin coupling L rewards neighbouring products στ that agree. At L = 0 the layers ignore each other: two copies of the Ising model, critical at the Onsager coupling K = 0.44069. At L = K the four states (σ, τ) = (±1, ±1) are all alike and the model is the four-state Potts modelA lattice model with q states per site, where neighbours gain energy only if they are in the same state. q = 2 is Ising.. In between, a dualityAn exact map between the model at strong and at weak coupling. If the transition is unique, it must sit where the map sends the model to itself: the self-dual locus. pins every critical point to one curve, the self-dual line. Drag along it in the trace above: the specific-heat exponentα/ν: how fast the specific-heat peak grows with system size, C ~ Lα/ν. ratio α/ν climbs from 0 at the Ising end to 1 at the Potts end, while γ/ν = 7/4 never moves. The symbols in the trace: yt is the thermal RG eigenvalue (how fast a temperature deviation grows under coarse-graining, ν = 1/yt); gR is the Coulomb-gas coupling, a number that labels the position on the line; α/ν and γ/ν say how the specific-heat and susceptibility peaks grow with size, C ~ Lα/ν and χ ~ Lγ/ν. The trace starts at s = 0.25 (a quarter of the way along, in the angle coordinate of the working equations). 1

The live toy: four colours for the four (σ, τ) states. It starts off the line, at K = 0.4, L = 0.05. Tick “lock onto the self-dual line” and slide the position; K and L follow the line. Unlock and move K or L freely to leave it. (Metropolis on a periodic lattice: a toy, not an instrument.)

Working equations

The Hamiltonian (the inverse temperature is absorbed into the couplings), and Wiseman and Domany's coordinates: 1

H = −Σ⟨ij⟩ [ Kσiσj + Kτiτj + Lσiσjτiτj ],   Z = e−4K,   X = e−2(K+L)

In the unit square of (X, Z): the parabola Z = X2 is L = 0 (decoupled Ising), the diagonal Z = X is L = K (four-state Potts), and duality places the critical line on Z = 1 − 2X. That is the same curve as the form Ginsparg prints, 5

Z = 1 − 2X  ⇔  e−4K = 1 − 2e−2K−2L  ⇔  2e−2Ke−2L = 1 − e−4K  ⇔  e−2L = (1 − e−4K)/(2e−2K) = ½(e2K − e−2K) = sinh 2K.

The critical segment runs from the Ising point X0 = (√2 − 1, 3 − 2√2) (K = 0.44069, L = 0) to the Potts point X4 = (1/3, 1/3) (K = L = ¼ ln 3 = 0.27465). Along it (Coulomb-gas results quoted by Wiseman and Domany): 1

yt = 2 − 2/gR,   gR = (8/π) arcsin[½ coth 2K],   α/ν = 2yt − 2,   γ/ν = 7/4.

On the line, coth 2K = (1 + Z)/(1 − Z) = (1 − X)/X, so the argument of the arcsine runs from 1/√2 (Ising: gR = 2, α/ν = 0) to 1 (Potts: gR = 4, α/ν = 1). The trace's slider moves uniformly in θ = arcsin[½ coth 2K], which makes gR = 8θ/π exactly linear (this page's arithmetic). Full derivation

Why duality locates the line. Kramers–Wannier duality maps the Ising model's high-temperature expansion onto the low-temperature expansion of a dual model. On the square lattice Aasen, Mong and Fendley print the self-dual (critical) line of the anisotropic model as 4

(sinh 2Jx)(sinh 2Jy) = 1   (their eq. 2.2).

For Jx = Jy = K this gives sinh 2K = 1, i.e. Kc = ½ ln(1 + √2) = 0.4407 (the number is this page's arithmetic; the paper prints only the equation, and its eq. 2.13 drops a factor of 2 relative to 2.2). A duality has a fixed set; if the model has a single transition, it must lie on that set. Ashkin–Teller has two couplings, so its duality map (K, L) → (K*, L*) has a fixed curve, e−2L = sinh 2K, as Wiseman–Domany and Ginsparg print it. The dual map itself is not reproduced here.

Where on the line. Ginsparg gives the position as a compact-boson radius on the orbifold line: sin(πr2/4) = ½ coth 2K, with r = 1 at Ising × Ising and r = √2 at four-state Potts. 5 Put that into Wiseman–Domany's gR: arcsin(sin(πr2/4)) = πr2/4 for r ≤ √2, so gR = 2r2 and yt = 2 − 1/r2. That is this page's combination of the two printed formulas; radius normalisations differ between sources, so treat the factor as convention-dependent.

The marginal operator. At the decoupled point the operator that moves you along the line is ε1ε2, the product of the two Ising energy operators: Ginsparg calls it “the Ashkin–Teller interaction coupling the two Ising energy operators”, the four-fermion term that “simply changes the radius of a free boson”. 5

2 Baxter's eight-vertex model: the textbook line

Put an arrow on every bond of a square lattice and allow only eight kinds of vertex: Baxter solved this exactly, and its exponents vary continuously. It is the textbook critical line, and Ashkin–Teller's exact cousin.

Click a bond to flip its arrow. Each vertex has four arrows round it. The ice ruleTwo arrows point in and two point out at every vertex, like the two near and two far hydrogens round each oxygen in ice. (two arrows in and two out at every vertex) allows six arrangements (types a, b, c); the eight-vertex model also allows the two in which all four arrows point in (a sink) or all point out (a source), called d. Arrangements with one or three arrows in are forbidden (red ×). Each type carries a weight, and the weight of a configuration is the product over its vertices. 6 The “flip a path” button flips a whole chain of bonds from one edge to another. That keeps the number of incoming arrows at every vertex even (its parity), because each vertex on the chain has exactly two of its arrows flipped; in six-vertex mode the chain follows the arrows, so each vertex also keeps exactly two in.

From the literature, not verified locally (Baxter's papers are not held here). The zero-field eight-vertex model with weights a, b, c, d is usually written with the anisotropy

Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2 − d2) / 2(ab + cd),

which reduces to the six-vertex Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2)/2ab when d = 0. Baxter (1971–72) solved it and found critical exponents that vary continuously along its critical manifold; Wegner (1972) and Fan & Wu related it to Ashkin–Teller, and Kadanoff & Brown (1979) to the Gaussian model. 20

The c = 1 picture (held, Ginsparg). All the critical theories with central charge c = 1 form a moduli space of two half-lines. The circle line is the Gaussian model; the orbifold line is “equivalent to the critical Ashkin–Teller model (which also encompasses … the 6-vertex model and the 8-vertex model on its critical line)”. They meet at one point, which is the continuum Kosterlitz–Thouless point of the XY model. On the orbifold line Ising × Ising sits at r = 1 and the four-state Potts model at r = √2. Three further c = 1 theories (T, O, I) are isolated points: c = 1 does not by itself imply a line. 5

3 Two Ising layers coupled bilinearly: the contrast

Keep the two Ising layers but couple them spin to spin (gσiτi) instead of product to product: dimension counting says this coupling grows under coarse-graining, so the line is expected (not shown here) to collapse back to a point.

Coarse-graining multiplies each small coupling by by, where b is the blocking factor and the RG eigenvaluey = d − x: the space dimension d minus the scaling dimension x of the operator the coupling multiplies. y > 0 grows (relevant), y < 0 dies (irrelevant), y = 0 is marginal. y = d − x (x is the scaling dimension; this page keeps Δ for the Blume–Capel crystal field and the XXZ anisotropy). An Ising spin has dimension 1/8 and the Ising energy density has dimension 1. The bilinear term gσiτi multiplies two spins: x = 1/4, y = 7/4, strongly relevant. Ashkin–Teller's four-spin term multiplies two energy densities: x = 2, y = 0, marginal. 22 Ginsparg identifies that product, ε1ε2, as the operator that moves the theory along the line. 5

Full derivation

When is a marginal operator exactly marginal? Ginsparg's criterion: a dimension-(1,1) operator V generates a one-parameter family of critical theories only if, to leading order, the V×V operator product contains no term CVVV(z − w)−1(z̄ − w̄)−1V. If it does, the two-point function picks up a logarithm and V's weight shifts to (1 − δg πCVVV, 1 − δg πCVVV): marginally relevant on one side, marginally irrelevant on the other. Higher orders require further integrals to vanish. 5

For Ashkin–Teller the check passes for a structural reason: on the critical line ε1ε2 = ψ1ψ̄1ψ2ψ̄2 is the four-fermion term of the massless Thirring model, which only changes the radius of a free boson. Changing a radius is a motion along a line, so no fixed point is left. 5 The bilinear σ1σ2 has no such protection: its y = 7/4 is far from zero, so the question of higher orders never arises.

4 O(n1)⊕O(n2): bicritical and tetracritical points

Two different order parameters, two critical lines that meet at one point: the sign of D = u1u2 − w2 decides whether the two orders coexist (tetracritical) or exclude each other (bicritical).

The plane shows the two “temperatures” r1 and r2, one per order parameter; each orders when its r turns negative. The self-interactions u1, u2 keep each order finite; the cross-coupling w decides how they meet. Slide w: with w2 < u1u2 a green mixed phase, where both orders coexist, opens between two critical lines (four lines meet: tetracritical); with w2 > u1u2 the orders repel and a first-order flop line separates them (bicritical). This is mean field, the Landau picture, computed live as the lowest of the candidate minima at every point. 10

The Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson Hamiltonian of Calabrese, Pelissetto and Vicari (φ1 has n1 components, φ2 has n2): 10

H = ∫ddx { ½[(∂φ1)2 + (∂φ2)2] + ½[r1φ12 + r2φ22] + (1/4!)[u1(φ12)2 + u2(φ22)2 + 2wφ12φ22] }

Mean field: D = u1u2 − w2 > 0 tetracritical, < 0 bicritical (D is this page’s letter for the discriminant, to keep Δ for the Blume–Capel crystal field and the XXZ anisotropy). Tetracritical: four continuous lines meet, bounding a mixed phase where both orders coexist. Bicritical: two continuous lines meet a first-order line, the flop line, across which the system jumps from one order to the other. The biconical fixed point is a third, non-symmetric fixed point where both couplings stay finite; the decoupled one has w = 0. Crossover exponents measure how slowly a nearly symmetric point is left. P4,4 is the spin-4 (quartic, fully anisotropic) perturbation of the O(N) point and φ4,4 its crossover exponent: at the O(N) point φ4,4 ≈ 0.01 for N = 3 (the crossover is practically invisible), ≈ 0.08 for N = 4 and ≈ 0.15 for N = 5. 10

What a real multicritical crossover looks like at finite size. In the fully frustrated XY model on the square lattice the chiral (Ising) and spin (KT) transitions are 1.6% apart. Hasenbusch, Pelissetto and Vicari, with L up to 1000, find the chiral exponent ν on a plateau near 0.8 for L ≲ ξs ≈ 118 and the Ising value 1 beyond it: “by using only data with L ≲ ξs one would obtain ν = 0.8 instead of the Ising value.” 11 Granato, Kosterlitz, Lee and Nightingale's coupled XY–Ising systems (1991) had reported exponents varying along one transition line. 12

Full derivation

The mean-field computation behind the graphic. Write a = φ12, b = φ22 ≥ 0 and F = ½(r1a + r2b) + (1/24)(u1a2 + u2b2 + 2wab). The four candidate minima are disordered (0, 0) with F = 0; phase 1 (a = −6r1/u1, b = 0) with F = −3r12/2u1; phase 2 likewise; and the mixed phase solving [u1 w; w u2](a, b) = −6(r1, r2), with F = ¼(r1a + r2b). A candidate that is not a minimum (a negative a or b, or a mixed stationary point that is a saddle because D < 0) has F = +∞; the phase is the candidate of lowest F. The phase-1 state is unstable to φ2 when r2 < (w/u1)r1, and phase 2 to φ1 when r1 < (w/u2)r2; the wedge between those lines exists exactly when w2 < u1u2. When D < 0, phases 1 and 2 have equal F on r1/√u1 = r2/√u2: the flop line. (This page's algebra on the printed Hamiltonian.)

The ε-expansion reversal. To low order (Nelson, Kosterlitz and Fisher; Fisher and Nelson) the symmetric O(N) point is the stable one for N < Nc = 4 − 2ε + O(ε2); above Nc the biconical and then the decoupled point take over as N grows. Naively continued to ε = 1 the leading term gives Nc ≈ 2, so O(3) would already be unstable; the low-order series cannot settle it. Five-loop ε and six-loop fixed-dimension series do: the spin-4 perturbation P4,4 is relevant for N ≥ 3, with Nc = 2.87(5) (< 2.9), so O(N) is stable only for N = 2, the biconical point takes over at N = 3 and the decoupled point at N ≥ 4. 10

5 Blume–Capel: a tricritical point

Ising spins that may also be missing (σ = 0), and a knob Δ that charges rent for every spin that is present: turn Δ up and the ordering temperature falls, until at one special place, the tricritical point, the transition turns abruptly first order.

Start here if this model is new. Picture a lattice where each site is either empty (σ = 0) or holds a little magnet pointing up or down (σ = ±1). Neighbouring magnets that agree lower the energy by J, as in the Ising model. Every magnet that is present costs Δ, the crystal fieldHere: a single-site energy Δσ2. With the plus sign used on this page, Δ > 0 makes nonzero spins expensive and so controls how many sites are empty.. Click the little lattice above to change sites, and drag Δ: at T = 0 a fully ordered site gains zJ/2 = 2 of bond energy (four neighbours, each bond shared) and pays Δ, so emptiness wins beyond Δ0 = 2. 13 The same competition at finite temperature produces the phase diagram below, the kind of diagram seen in 3He–4He mixtures.

Working equations

Zierenberg, Fytas, Weigel, Janke and Malakis (2017) mapped the boundary by crossing it at fixed T while varying Δ, with multicanonical simulations, and used ξ/L at the size crossings as the universality test (it must reach the exact Ising value 0.905 on the continuous part). Their Hamiltonian (J = kB = 1; here Δ is the crystal field, not the XXZ anisotropy of §7): 13

H = −JΣ⟨ij⟩σiσj + ΔΣiσi2 = EJ + ΔEΔ,   σi ∈ {−1, 0, +1}.

Δ → −∞ removes the vacancies and gives the Ising model; Δ = 0 orders at T0 ≈ 1.69; the boundary reaches T = 0 at Δ0 = zJ/2 = 2. On the second-order part the exponents are Ising: α = 0, β = 1/8, γ = 7/4, ν = 1; at T = 1.2 the paper measures ν = 0.97(10) and γ/ν = 1.750(3), and a logarithmic specific-heat peak. At T = 0.5 (first order) the pseudo-critical fields shift as L−2 and the peaks grow as L2, the volume.

The tricritical point. Quoted literature values (Δt, Tt): Beale (1986) (1.9655(50), 0.610(5)); Wilding and Nielaba (1996) (1.9665(3), 0.608(1)); Kwak et al. (2015) (1.9660(1), 0.6080(1)), the most precise. The tricritical exponents are not printed in the held source, and this page does not quote them.

The approach. Far above Tt the universal ratio ξ/L at the crossings extrapolates to the exact Ising value 0.905; approaching Tt “additional corrections emerge”, the crossings depart from 0.905, and a fit at T = 0.65 with L ≥ 64 still has poor quality. The paper reads this as the crossover to tricritical scaling; it does not measure a crossover exponent. Finite-size forms used:

first order: Δ*L = Δ* + aL−D;   second order: Δ*L = Δc + aL−1/ν, C*L ~ ln L, χ*L ~ Lγ/ν.
Full derivation

Why a tricritical point needs two knobs (schematic Landau argument, no numbers). Expand the free energy in the order parameter m: F = a m2 + b m4 + c m6, with c > 0. If b > 0 the minimum leaves m = 0 continuously when a changes sign: a second-order line, one condition (a = 0). If b < 0 the m6 term holds the order finite and m jumps: first order. The two behaviours meet where a = 0 and b = 0: two conditions, so two knobs, here T and Δ. The vacancies are what make b change sign: diluting the magnets weakens the effective quartic stiffness. (Standard Landau theory; not checked here against a held source.)

6 Potts q = 5, 6: walking

With five or more states per site the 2D Potts transition is first order, but so weakly that any lattice smaller than the correlation length looks critical: the flow walks slowly past a pair of fixed points that have moved into the complex plane.

Slide your lattice size L. A first-order transition shows itself (two coexisting phases, a latent heat) only once L is larger than the correlation length ξ. For q = 10, ξ is about ten lattice spacings; for q = 5 it is about 2512. 14 16 Below that size the system imitates a critical point, and a standard analysis returns exponents with small error bars that are simply wrong. Gorbenko, Rychkov and Zan explain why: as q passes 4, the critical and tricritical fixed points collide and move off into the complex plane as a conjugate pair. The real flow passes between them; it slows down there without stopping, and then leaves.

Potts toy with the energy histogram below it. Press “go to Tc(q)” (Tc = 1/ln(1 + √q)) and run. When the lattice is larger than ξ(q), a long run near Tc can show two energy peaks; at q = 5 no lattice you can run here is large enough. 16

The drifting exponent: the effective thermal dimension δε changes like a tangent in ln r, so the “exponent” ν(r) you measure depends on the scale you measure it at. The amplitude is read off Gorbenko–Rychkov–Zan's Fig. 12 for q = 5 and is illustrative. 15

The fixed points are complex CFTs; at q = 5 the central charge is c ≈ 1.138 ± 0.021i. 15 Along the real flow two-point functions carry a drifting dimension (their eq. 4.33) and a drifting exponent

δφ(r) = ReΔφ − (√3ε/2π) Cφφε′ tan(ε ln(r/r0)/π),   ν(r) = 1/(d − δε(r)),   ε = √(q − 4).

The deceptive collapse. Iino, Morita, Kawashima and Sandvik simulated q = 5 up to L = 256. The best data collapse gives β = 0.070(8), ν = 0.61(5), with statistical errors smaller than the symbols; the true first-order values are β = 0, ν = 1/d = 0.5. From (L/2, L) crossings, ν(L) and β(L) drift with L; a power-law extrapolation of 1/ν(L) returns 1/ν(∞) ≈ 2.23 > d, impossible for a continuous transition. The shadow. 7 XXZ and the six-vertex model

A chain of quantum spins whose z-coupling Δ you can dial: for every Δ between −1 and +1 the chain is critical, so a whole segment of Δ is one critical line. The same physics can be drawn as arrows on a 2D lattice, the six-vertex model.

Start here if this model is new. Line up spin-½ particles, each up or down. The Hamiltonian has two kinds of term. The XX + YY part takes a neighbouring up–down pair and swaps it: think of an up spin as a particle that hops one site (the arcs above). The ΔZZ part only looks: it adds an energy for each neighbouring pair depending on whether the two agree. Hopping wants the spins to spread and fluctuate; Δ wants them to line up (one sign) or alternate (the other). For |Δ| < 1 neither wins: the chain stays gaplessExcitations cost arbitrarily little energy in a long chain, and correlations decay as powers of distance: the chain is critical. for every value in the range. With this page’s sign, Δ > 0 favours neighbours that agree along z (toward the ferromagnet at Δ = +1) and Δ < 0 favours neighbours that alternate (toward the Néel antiferromagnet at Δ = −1). 7 ⚠ sign convention: H = −JΣ[…], so Δ = +1 is the ferromagnet, Δ = −1 the antiferromagnetThis page follows Franchini's H = −JΣ[…]: Δ = +1 is the ferromagnet and Δ = −1 the antiferromagnet. Many texts write H = +JΣ[…], which reverses the sign of Δ and swaps the names of the two ends.

The six-vertex builder: only ice-rule vertices are allowed. Set the weights a, b, c and watch Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2)/2ab move the point across Reshetikhin's three regimes; the circle a2 + b2 = c2 is the free-fermion point Δ = 0. 6

Exponents along the line. One number, the Luttinger parameter K, sets them all: xm,n = m2/(4K) + n2K. 9 As Δ moves, K moves and so do the exponents; the marginal operator ∂X∂̄X (the one that changes the radius) keeps dimension 2.

For the Compass

One end (Δ = −1 here) is a BKT-type point, with all the treachery of the KT page's finite-size section; the other is a ferromagnet. c = 1 all along the line, so a transfer-matrix measurement of c cannot say WHERE on the line you are; the exponents can. ◆ design 21

The chain in Franchini's convention (S = σ/2, periodic): 7

In the critical regime, Δ = −cos γ (0 < γ < π), the sound velocity is vS = π sin γ/(2γ) and the Luttinger parameter is K = π/(2(π − γ)) = π/(2 arccos Δ): K = 1/2 at Δ = −1, 1 at Δ = 0, and → ∞ as Δ → +1. The staggered Sz correlation decays as r−2K. The saturation field is hs = (J/2)(1 − Δ). 7 In Ueda and Oshikawa's notation the vertex operator with charges (m, n) has dimension xm,n = m2/(4K) + n2K, 9 so the staggered Sz (n = 1) has 2x = 2K, matching Franchini, and the transverse spin (m = 1) has 2x = 1/(2K). Ginsparg writes the compact-boson dimensions in the same shape with a radius, m2/(4r2) + n2r2 (the r–K dictionary depends on normalisation; not checked here). 5

The six-vertex side. Weights a (a1, a2), b (b1, b2), c (c1, c2); R-matrix [[a,0,0,0],[0,b,c,0],[0,c,b,0],[0,0,0,a]]; Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2)/2ab. Transfer matrices with equal Δ commute, and the transfer matrix is “the generating function for [the] commuting family of local Hamiltonians”: H1 = Σ(σxσx + σyσy + Δσzσz), printed with an overall plus sign. 6 At zero field Reshetikhin’s regimes are “ferromagnetic” (frozen, Δ > 1), “disordered” (|Δ| < 1, power-law correlations) and “antiferromagnetic” (Δ < −1), with the free-fermion point at Δ = 0. (In nonzero fields frozen phases occur for every Δ, and a disordered region also for Δ > 1.) Franchini states the dictionary explicitly (his App. B): Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2)/2ab (B.31); a = ρ sinh(λ + φ), b = ρ sinh λ, c = ρ sinh φ, Δ = cosh φ (B.33); and d ln T/dλ at λ = 0 is the XXZ chain plus a constant (B.45), up to the sign conventions noted above. 7 A special six-vertex model, the F-model (= the BCSOS surface model), is rigorously of KT type and served Hasenbusch, Marcu and Pinn as their exact reference flow. 8

Full derivation

The Bethe ansatz in one screen (Franchini, ch. 4 and App. C). 7 Start from the all-up state and add M down spins. Bethe's guess: the eigenstate is a sum over permutations of plane waves, eiΣkjnj, with an amplitude that changes only by a two-body phase when two magnons pass. Parametrising the momenta by rapidities, eik = sin((φ/2)(λ + i))/sin((φ/2)(λ − i)), the phase depends only on rapidity differences provided cosh φ = Δ. Periodicity gives M coupled Bethe equations. In the planar regime φ becomes imaginary, φ = iγ′, so Δ = cos γ′; Franchini’s Table 4.1 writes the same regime with γ = π − γ′, which is the Δ = −cos γ used in the working equations, and in the thermodynamic limit the ground state is a filled sea of real rapidities with a smooth density. The finite-size spectrum then has the form of a c = 1 Gaussian theory, ΔE = (2πvS/L)[(ΔN)2/(4Z2) + (Zd)2 + N+ + N−], where Z is the dressed charge at the edge of the sea. Comparing with the Gaussian form identifies K = Z2; a Wiener–Hopf evaluation at vanishing field gives K = π/(2(π − γ)).

8 Networks that find their own critical manifold

A network that rewires itself finds the edge between silence and runaway activity, then keeps changing its structure while staying on that edge (2023); with two kinds of transition, it can drift along the Hopf manifold into the Turing one (2025).

Each node is inactive, firing or refractory. A firing node excites each inactive out-neighbour at rate β, stops firing at rate δ and recovers at rate γ; now and then an inactive node fires on its own. The adaptive rule is homeostatic: a firing node loses an incoming link at rate l, and new links appear between random nodes at rate g. Too many links and activity runs away, so links are cut; too few and activity dies, so links accumulate. The system settles where the network's leading eigenvalue λ1The largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix: how strongly the network amplifies activity. Activity persists when λ1 exceeds the threshold λ1*. sits at the threshold: the transition in 2023 is the onset of activity. The toy runs N = 200 nodes with sped-up adaptation, so its trace is noisier than the paper's N = 105; watch λ1 climb and then hover near the cyan line while ⟨k⟩ rises past it and the in/out-degree correlation ρ turns negative. 18

2025, schematic (a different model from the toy above: here the eigenvalues are of the network Laplacian; the toy’s λ1 is an adjacency eigenvalue): two local plasticity rules act on different parts of the network (the Hopf rule on well-connected nodes, the Turing rule on weakly connected ones), so the system can hold one condition while it drifts into the other. 19

10 What to remember, and the final test

Ten lines to keep, the names to recognise, and a 25-item test drawn fresh on every attempt.

  1. Ashkin–Teller: self-dual line sinh 2K = e−2L (Z = 1 − 2X) from Ising to four-state Potts; α/ν = 2yt − 2 climbs 0 → 1, γ/ν = 7/4 fixed.
  2. The Potts end carries multiplicative logs (Salas–Sokal); a LINE point can look first order at reachable L (Jin–Sen–Sandvik).
  3. Coupling type decides geometry: ε1ε2 (x = 2, y = 0) makes a line; σ1σ2 (x = 1/4, y = 7/4) makes a point.
  4. Baxter's eight-vertex model: the exactly solved line. c = 1: a circle (Gaussian) line and an orbifold (critical AT) line meeting at the KT point (Ginsparg).
  5. O(n1)⊕O(n2): D = u1u2 − w2 > 0 tetracritical, < 0 bicritical; in d = 3 the O(N) point is stable only for N = 2 (Calabrese–Pelissetto–Vicari).
  6. Blume–Capel: H = −JΣσσ′ + ΔΣσ2; T = 0 end at Δ0 = 2; tricritical at (1.9660(1), 0.6080(1)); two knobs.
  7. Potts q > 4: weakly first order; ξ(5) ≈ 2512, ξ(6) ≈ 159; walking between complex fixed points; drifting ν(r) = 1/(d − δε(r)).
  8. XXZ (Franchini's sign): critical for |Δ| < 1, c = 1, K = π/(2 arccos Δ); Δ = −1 is the BKT-type end.
  9. Six-vertex Δ = (a2 + b2 − c2)/2ab; equal Δ ⇒ commuting transfer matrices ⇒ the chain.
  10. Sormunen, Gross & Saramäki: λ1* = δ/β + (δ + γ/2)/(δ + γ); drift along one universality class is invisible to exponents.
