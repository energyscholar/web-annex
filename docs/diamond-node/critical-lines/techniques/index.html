Critical Lines — the Techniques

How an instrument sees a line.

The models page learns systems. This page learns instruments: the ways a measurement can tell a critical line from an isolated critical point from a transition that only pretends to be critical, and when it must decline to say. Each section names what a technique needs, what it decides, what it is blind to, what it costs, how it reads in the language of the relational kernel, and which side of the firewall it lives on.

The alphabet these pages share (the Criticality Compass’s verdicts ◆ design)1

POINT
an isolated fixed point; the RG flow stops at one place, with one escape direction (Ising).
LINE
a continuum of fixed points; the flow stops wherever it lands; an exactly marginal direction (the Ashkin–Teller self-dual line; the XXZ/six-vertex critical regime; the XY low-temperature phase).
WALKING
the flow slows to a small nonzero minimum, never stops, then leaves; finite systems imitate criticality (Potts q = 5, 6).
REFUSE
the honest verdict when the data cannot tell (the default cell).

The organising idea is follow the flow: the velocity, accumulation and circulation of the flow over declared scale and control relations are what the Detector reads. The Detector classifies a system’s critical geometry from observations; the Navigator finds a critical line and moves along it.

How to read: every section shows a headline, one live graphic and a cyan For the Compass box. “Working equations” opens the physicist’s layer; “Full derivation” inside it opens the deepest one. “Check yourself” holds three graded items; the final test at the end is retakeable and draws new items each time. Scores stay in this browser. The mark ◆ design flags a design choice of the instrument, never a physics result.

0 Follow the flow

An instrument never sees a fixed point directly; it sees the flow, and the flow offers three readings: how fast it moves (velocity), how far it has gone (accumulation), and whether it turns (circulation).

1 The linearised RG matrix (a refresher; the long version is the RG page)

Near a fixed point the flow is a matrix: its eigenvalues are the exponents, its eigenvectors are the directions, and an eigenvalue of exactly 1 (y = 0) that stays exactly 1 beyond linear order makes a whole line of fixed points.

Kadanoff’s idea was to throw detail away on purpose by blocking spins; Wilson made the blocking a map on couplings and found the exponents in its fixed points.2 Close to a fixed point the map is linear, δK′ = M δK, and everything is in the eigenvalues, written λ = by for a scale factor b: a relevanty > 0 (λ > 1): the deviation grows under coarse-graining; it must be tuned to zero to sit on the critical point. direction grows, an irrelevanty < 0 (λ < 1): the deviation shrinks and is forgotten; this is universality. one shrinks, and a marginaly = 0 (λ = 1): at linear order the deviation neither grows nor shrinks. If it stays exactly marginal at every order, the fixed point is one of a line. one does neither. Drag the four entries of M. The arrows are the step δK′ − δK; the dotted orbit is repeated application of M. Set an eigenvalue to 1 and the fixed line appears; give M a complex pair and the orbit turns.

Full derivation

The worked example: 1D Ising decimation.2 Sum out every second spin of a chain with coupling K:

Σσ2=±1 eK(σ1σ2 + σ2σ3) = 2 cosh(K(σ1 + σ3)) = eg + K′σ1σ3

Taking σ1σ3 = +1 gives 2 cosh 2K = eg + K′; taking −1 gives 2 = eg − K′. Divide: e2K′ = cosh 2K, so

K′ = ½ ln cosh 2K,    b = 2.

Fixed points: K* = 0 and K* = ∞, nothing between. Linearise at K* = 0: dK′/dK = tanh 2K → 0, so λ = 0 < 1: the high-temperature fixed point is fully attractive (a phase, not a transition). At large K, K′ ≈ K − ½ ln 2: the flow creeps down by a constant each step, so K* = ∞ is unstable, and the chain has no finite-temperature transition. A 1 × 1 “matrix” already shows the whole logic: fixed points, eigenvalues, stability.

2 Monte Carlo RG: the instrument

Coarse-grain the same snapshots again and again: the change from one level to the next is the flow, and a single lattice gives several RG steps.

Swendsen (1979) turned Kadanoff’s picture into a measurement: simulate at the candidate critical point, block the snapshots, block the blocks, and compare levels.3 The toy is a 64×64 Ising lattice (32×32 on a narrow screen) with Metropolis updates. Each smaller panel is a 2×2 majority-ruleA block spin takes the sign of the sum of its four spins; a tie takes the block’s top-left spin (a fixed rule, so the map is deterministic). blocking of the one before, labelled with its nearest-neighbour correlation, and the chart above plots those four numbers (nn = the nearest-neighbour correlation ⟨sisj⟩, averaged over neighbouring pairs). Run it and move T. Near Tc = 2/ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269 (the arithmetic is in §5) the levels look alike and their correlations nearly agree: a fixed point. Below it the blocks order level by level; above it they disorder. That drift is the flow, read from one lattice.

Everything on the right of (4) and (5) is a correlation measured in the simulation, so (3) is a linear system for T. Blöte and co-workers write the same thing as 𝔅T = ℭ (their eq. 5; matching it to (3)–(5) makes 𝔅 the level-(n+1) auto-correlations and ℭ the cross-correlations, our reading), and solve it after truncating to a finite set of couplings; the number of couplings used in the literature rose from 7 (1979) to 99 (1992).5 The eigenvalues of T give y = ln λ / ln b with b = 2.2

The block rule is a choice. Ron, Brandt & Swendsen use a tunable rule, equation (6):

P(σ′l) = exp(w σ′l Σj∈l σj) / [exp(w Σj∈l σj) + exp(−w Σj∈l σj)]   (6)

which is the majority rule at w = ∞. Tuned to w = 0.4314 (even sector) and 0.555 (odd sector), with 30 even and 20 odd couplings on the 3D Ising model, it gives yT1 = 1.591(1) and yH1 = 2.4829(2), converged by the second or third iteration.4 (The even sector holds couplings unchanged when every spin is flipped, such as nearest-neighbour products, and gives yT; the odd sector holds those that change sign, such as the field, and gives yH.)

The surprise. The eigenvalues converge while the renormalised correlation functions do not: “there is no evidence that the tuned RG transformation brings the renormalized Hamiltonians closer to the fixed point.”4 Blöte et al. (1996) had already shown a nearly flat approach for a tuned Hamiltonian and rule: yT = 1.5885 → 1.5852 → 1.5829 and yH = 2.48492 → 2.48309 → 2.48219 over n = 1–3 at L = 32.5 In two dimensions the plain majority rule already converges by the second iteration.4 Why it matters for the instrument: the page says “watch the flow”, and the surprise says the eigenvalues (the flow’s local rates) can settle while the position on the trajectory has not. The Detector therefore never trusts a settled eigenvalue: it reads the per-level differences, each with its declared band, through the five-valued sign (§3), level by level, and a zero is claimed only where the sign reads ZERO! at every declared level.

How MCRG sees the alphabet ◆ design:19 POINT: one eigenvalue above 1, the rest below, and levels n and n+1 agree. LINE: one eigenvalue that stays at exactly 1 across levels, and fixed-level statistics that depend on where the run started. WALKING: an eigenvalue near 1 that drifts; level differences reach a small nonzero minimum, then grow.

Full derivation

Write the level-n weight as exp(Σβ Kβ(n)Sβ(n)) (Ron, Brandt & Swendsen put no minus sign in the exponent, and neither do we here).4 For any quantity Q computed from a configuration, ∂⟨Q⟩/∂Kβ = ⟨Q Sβ⟩ − ⟨Q⟩⟨Sβ⟩, because ⟨Q⟩ = Z−1Σ Q eΣKS and differentiating the numerator brings down Sβ, differentiating Z−1 brings down −⟨Sβ⟩.

Take Q = Sγ(n+1), the block operator computed from the blocked configuration. Averaged over the level-n ensemble it is a function of the K(n); averaged over the level-(n+1) ensemble it is a function of the K(n+1). The blocking rule makes the two averages the same number, so ⟨Sγ(n+1)⟩ depends on K(n) only through K(n+1)(K(n)). The chain rule is (3). Applying the identity above in each ensemble gives (4) (Q = S′, derivative with respect to a level-n coupling, so the partner operator is S(n)) and (5) (derivative with respect to a level-(n+1) coupling, partner S(n+1)). In matrix form (3) is (4) = (5) T, so T = (5)−1(4); with one coupling, T is the ratio of two numbers, which is what the trace computes live.

3 Drifting exponents, finite-size scaling, and the five-valued sign

At one size LINE, WALKING and BKT look alike; how an exponent moves tells them apart.

They differ in how an effective exponent changes with SCALE (the system size, or the RG level) and with CONTROL (a knob such as the temperature or a coupling, varied between runs), and reading that difference honestly needs a margin ε declared before the data.

Finite-size scaling reads exponents from how observables change with the system size L. Iino, Morita, Kawashima and Sandvik showed how that goes wrong: the q = 5 Potts transition is first order, yet sizes L = 24–256 collapse beautifully with β = 0.070(8), ν = 0.61(5) and error bars smaller than the symbols.7 The cure is to follow the exponent: from crossings of size pairs (L/2, L) they read a size-dependent ν(L) and β(L), whose power-law extrapolation gives 1/ν(∞) ≈ 2.23, larger than 2, which is impossible for a continuous transition in two dimensions (by hyperscaling, α = 2 − dν, it would mean α > 1, beyond even the first-order value ν = 1/d).17 Gorbenko, Rychkov and Zan explain the drift: along a walking flow scaling dimensions drift with scale in a tangent-shaped way.8 At a BKT point the drift is different again: finite-size values approach their limits only as 1/(ln L + C).10

The four sequences above are synthetic, built with those shapes. Each panel shows an effective exponent against log2 L at two control values. Sort them: which varies with the control but not with scale, which varies with neither, which drifts faster and faster, which slows but never settles?

  • POINT: the exponent varies with neither SCALE nor CONTROL (runs at the critical point, on its critical surface).
  • LINE: it varies with CONTROL but not with SCALE.
  • WALKING: it drifts with SCALE, faster and faster as the flow leaves.
  • BKT: it slows with SCALE but never settles, approaching its limit as 1/(ln L + C).

Reading a drift needs a verdict per step, and that is where a five-valued sign comes in ◆ design.1 Let v be a velocity estimate (a per-level difference) and w the half-width of its band, so the band is [v − w, v + w]; it is read against a declared margin ±ε:

The cell is looked up in a table with a row for every combination of four yes/no tests: a = the whole band lies above +ε (v − w > ε); b = it lies below −ε (v + w < −ε); c = it lies inside ±ε (|v| + w < ε); r = it excludes zero. The four answers form the key abcr (1 = yes). Some keys cannot occur for w, ε ≥ 0 (a and b together would need a negative band); they are given the cell ? so that the table is total and no input falls through it. In words: c without r (0010) is ZERO!; c with r (0011) is SLOW by the overlap rule; r alone (0001) is SLOW; a with r (1001) is SIG+; b with r (0101) is SIG−; every other key is ?. No chain of conditions decides the cell. Where ZERO! and SLOW could both be claimed (the band lies inside ±ε and excludes zero), the cell is SLOW: the overlap resolves toward the weaker claim, and a LINE is never asserted on an ambiguous reading. With ε = 0 the table collapses to the three-valued sign (SIG+, SIG−, ?). The margin plays the role of the equivalence bounds of a TOSTTwo one-sided tests: reject “the effect is at or below −Δ” and reject “it is at or above +Δ”; together they support “inside the bounds”. The bounds are declared first. procedure, which Lakens insists are declared before the data are seen: “no significant difference” is not equivalence.11

Full derivation

Why the doubling chain fails the design bound. A finite-size-scaling chain simulates at L, 2L, 4L, …. The work per independent sample grows like Ld+z, with z the dynamic exponent of the update: Ld sites per sweep, and about Lz sweeps between independent samples. Each doubling therefore multiplies the cost by 2d+z; for d = 2 and an illustrative z = 2 that is 16, and five doublings cost about 165 ≈ 106 times the first size. Separating LINE from WALKING needs L to approach ξ, which is 2512 for q = 5. That is exponential cost in the number of doublings, and it is why the instrument follows the flow within one lattice (§2) rather than across lattices.19

The ε = 0 limit. With ε = 0, c needs |v| + w < 0, impossible for w ≥ 0, so ZERO! is empty; SLOW needs r without a or b, and with ε = 0 r implies a or b, so SLOW is empty too. What remains is SIG+, SIG− and ?: the three-valued sign exactly.1

4 The kernel lens: A, B and R over declared relations

Declare the relations first; then three definitions turn exponent estimates into signs a table can read.

The three are a directed difference (A), an accumulation over immediate successors (B) and an antisymmetric response (R). R(g)[e] = g[e] + ρ (Σsucc g − Σpred g) is an antisymmetric response over a chain of declared relations: the kernel’s discrete analogue of circulation, not a loop integral (the circulation of §0 is the continuum picture it is an analogue of). An interior locus is a node with both a declared predecessor and a declared successor, s → t → u; B and R need one.

The relational kernel is a small set of operator definitions with one rule above them: nothing enters an operator undeclared.12 The chain is observable → M → a numerical projection (numbers) → declared relations → operators → result → comparison with observables, where M is the declared mapping from what was observed to the numbers the operators are applied to. Here the nodes are exponent estimates at RG levels 0–4; the edges are declared relations between them. Click an edge to declare or undeclare it, press a preset, or type your own values.

  • A (also written Δ): A(x)[e] = x[s] − x[t] over each declared edge e = s → t. It is the only step from a NodeField to an EdgeField.
  • B: B(g)[e] = g[e] + Σ g[f] over the immediate successors f of e. A terminal edge accumulates nothing.
  • R: R(g)[e] = g[e] + ρ[src(e)] (Σsucc g − Σpred g), with ρ = ρbase χ/(χ0 + χ) and χ = max|A| at the node. ρbase and χ0 are declared through M, e.g. ρbase = 0.5, χ0 = 0.1 (illustrative values, the graphic’s); the kernel holds no defaults.
  • C is not a kernel operator: it is a declared projection that states what it keeps and what it discards.

A missing declaration does not become zero: the operator is NOT EVALUATED, and that is a value the verdict table can read. Declare a closing edge (a ring) and the whole relation set becomes inadmissible: B’s accumulation needs a well-founded successor order, and on a ring a node would be its own predecessor. Tick “second control value” and the two relation families appear: Continuation over scale (along each row) and Coupling over control (between the rows). Full derivation

Two gates. The kernel reference names four verification stages: declaration (the domain, M, the relations and their provenance), mathematical (only declared operators, no imported constructs), observable validation (against independent observables, with no tuning) and repeatability.12 Passing them says the computation is traceable: every number follows from declared observables through declared operators. It does not say the physics is right. A block rule that throws away the relevant operator can be declared perfectly and still hide a LINE; a physically sound estimate built with an undeclared smoothing step can be correct and still fail conformance. So the instrument is checked twice: once for conformance and once against the exact routes of §5, and a pass at one gate says nothing about the other.

Why B needs an interior locus. B adds each edge’s immediate successors. On a chain s → t with nothing after t, the only edge is terminal and B(g) = g: nothing is accumulated, so B carries no information beyond A. Only when some node has both a declared incoming and a declared outgoing edge does B differ from A, which is why the reference makes that declaration the condition for the A → B → R path.

5 Exact routes: truth for the scoring side

When the Hamiltonian is known, three exact tools, duality, the transfer matrix and partition-function zeros, locate and classify a transition; they generate truth for scoring and never touch the instrument’s inputs.

The transfer matrix. Build the matrix that adds one row to an infinitely long Ising strip of width W, periodic across the width (2W states). Its largest eigenvalue gives the free energy per site f(W). At a conformally invariant critical point the leading finite-width correction is universal, f(W) = f∞ − πc/(6W2), where c is the central charge (Blöte, Cardy and Nightingale; Affleck).15 The demo computes W = 2 to 10 in your browser. Step through the trace and read c off the slope; then move K away from Kc and watch the estimate stop converging.

Duality. Kramers and Wannier found that the square-lattice Ising model maps onto itself with high and low temperature exchanged; the self-dual point is the transition. Aasen, Mong and Fendley print the anisotropic version as a whole critical, self-dual line, (sinh 2Jx)(sinh 2Jy) = 1.13 Slide Jx along it, then find the isotropic point: sinh 2K = 1 gives Kc = ½ ln(1 + √2) ≈ 0.4407. (That number is this page’s arithmetic from their equation (2.2); they do not print it.)

A line of critical points is not a LINE of fixed points. Every point of that curve is critical, and every point is the same Ising class (Onsager’s exact solution of the anisotropic model, not held here): a line of critical points with no varying exponent. Strictly, a blocking that keeps the anisotropy has a fixed point for each ratio Jy/Jx, but they differ only by rescaling one axis (a redundant direction: a change of the couplings that only rescales a coordinate and changes no universal quantity), so an eigenvalue of 1 along it is not a LINE. The primer’s LINE is an exactly marginal direction along which universal quantities vary; a redundant direction changes none, so the anisotropic Ising curve is not a LINE.

For Ashkin–Teller, duality answers with a genuine LINE: the self-dual line Z = 1 − 2X, along which the exponents vary.14

Partition-function zeros. Z of a finite system is a polynomial in e−β or in the field, so it has zeros, none of them on the positive real axis (the physical values), because every term of Z is positive. As L grows they pinch the axis, and the rate carries the order of the transition: the first zero approaches like L−1/ν at a continuous transition and like L−d at a first-order one (Janke and Kenna).17 Lee and Yang proved that for a ferromagnet the zeros in the complex plane of z = e−2βh (h the field) lie exactly on the unit circle.18

For the Compass

These are truth generators for the controls. None of them may touch the instrument’s inputs (the firewall): they need the Hamiltonian. The transfer matrix scores LINE (c constant and a marginal operator), duality locates lines exactly, and zeros at one control value decide the ORDER of a transition, not whether it sits on a LINE (that needs ν at several control values).19

Working equations

Kramers–Wannier as Aasen, Mong & Fendley print it.13 The critical, self-dual line is (2.2) (sinh 2Jx)(sinh 2Jy) = 1. With the weights e2Jy = cot uV and e2Jx = cot(π/4 − uH), the critical line is uH = uV and the isotropic critical point is uH = uV = π/8. The duality relation printed as (2.13), sinh J sinh Ĵ = 1 (Ĵ the coupling on the corresponding bond of the dual lattice), lacks the factor 2 that (2.2) carries: a slip in the paper, not in this page. In plain words: duality can be done on part of the lattice only, and the seam between the two parts is a line-like object, a defect, that can be moved freely without changing Z. In symbols it is Dσ with Dσσzσz = μxDσ and Dσσx = μzμzDσ, which fuses as Dσ2 = 1 + Dψ and is not invertible.

Ashkin–Teller. With Z = e−4K and X = e−2(K+L), the self-dual line Z = 1 − 2X runs from the Ising end to the 4-state Potts end, and the thermal exponent yt = 2 − 2/gR varies along it.14 That line has c = 1: it is the orbifold branch of the c = 1 compact boson.25

Walking in the strip. For q = 5 Potts the complex fixed point has c ≈ 1.138 ± 0.021i (Gorbenko, Rychkov & Zan, (3.2)),8 Re c = 1.13755. Ma and He measure c(L) on quantum Potts chains: at q = 5 it approaches Re c (1.149 at L = 20, 1.139 at L = 70), and the drift fits a tangent form c(L) = cR − α tan(γ ln(L/L0)) better than a polynomial in 1/L.16 A strip free-energy estimate of c is expected to show the same plateau near Re c at W ≪ ξ (Ma and He’s c is from the entanglement entropy; the strip version is not checked here).

Zeros, as Janke & Kenna write them.17

second order: rj(L) ~ ((2j − 1)/Ld)1/(νd) ~ L−1/ν  (2.14);    first order: rj(L) ~ L−d  (2.17) GL(rj) = (2j − 1)/(2Ld) = a1 rja2 + a3  (3.1–3.2)

Here rj is the distance of the j-th zero from the transition point, GL is the fraction of zeros closer than rj (the integrated density of zeros), and a1, a2, a3 are fit constants: a1 an amplitude, a2 the power, a3 an offset. Read the fit: a2 ≈ 1 means first order, with the latent heat set by the slope a1; a2 > 1 means second order with α = 2 − a2; a3 ≠ 0 means no transition. The first-order rate L−d is “the formal identification of ν with 1/d”. Fisher zeros live in the complex temperature plane at zero field; Lee–Yang zeros in the complex field plane. Whether the zeros of a walking system approach at a drifting rate is reasoned, not found in a source here.19

Full derivation

The strip transfer matrix, symmetrised. Let s = (s1…sW) be one row, periodic across the width. Split the Boltzmann weight into a row part D(s) = exp(K Σi sisi+1) and a between-row part V(s, s′) = exp(K Σi sis′i). Then T = D1/2 V D1/2 is symmetric, and V is a tensor product of W copies of the 2×2 matrix [[eK, e−K], [e−K, eK]], so applying T to a vector costs W·2W operations rather than 4W. For an M-row strip Z = tr TM → λmaxM, so the free energy per site in units of kBT is f(W) = −ln λmax/W. Power iteration finds λmax. Two widths give c: c = (6/π) [f(W) − f(W′)] / [1/W′2 − 1/W2]. This construction is standard; the code on this page is its direct transcription.

The Lee–Yang circle theorem, Theorem 3 of their Paper II (verbatim): “If the interaction u between two gas atoms is such that u = +∞ if the two atoms occupy the same lattice [site] and u ≤ 0 otherwise, then all the roots of the polynomial 𝒫 lie on the unit circle in the complex z-plane.” In the Ising language the variable of the theorem is z = e−2βh (proportional to the lattice-gas fugacity), so the ferromagnet’s field-plane zeros sit on a circle, and the transition is where they pinch the positive real axis. The theorem assumes nothing about the range of u, the dimension, or the lattice.18

8 What to remember, and the final test

Ten lines to keep, a 25-item final test that draws new items on every retake, a name drill, and your scores.

  1. The instrument follows the flow: velocity (A over scale), accumulation (B), circulation (R).
  2. Exponents are eigenvalues of the linearised RG matrix, λ = by; an eigenvalue exactly 1 is a line of fixed points.
  3. MCRG (Swendsen 1979): block the same snapshots again and again; Tαβ = ∂K′α/∂Kβ from two correlation matrices and the chain rule.
  4. Converged eigenvalues are not a converged trajectory (Ron, Brandt & Swendsen 2017); truncation can, by our reasoning, manufacture drift.
  5. A clean data collapse can be confidently wrong (Iino et al.: ν = 0.61(5) at a first-order transition).
  6. LINE varies with control, not scale; WALKING drifts with scale (Gorbenko–Rychkov–Zan (4.34)); POINT varies with neither; BKT approaches as 1/(ln L + C).
  7. The five-valued sign: SIG+, SIG−, ZERO!, SLOW, ?; ε declared first (TOST, Lakens); overlap goes to SLOW.
  8. Kernel V8: A = x[s] − x[t]; B(g)[e] = g[e] + Σ over immediate successors; R = g + ρ(Σsucc − Σpred); a missing declaration is NOT EVALUATED, never 0.
  9. F(L) = F∞ − πc/(6L2) (Blöte–Cardy–Nightingale; Affleck); sinh 2K = 1 at the Ising self-dual point (Kramers–Wannier).
  10. Zeros approach as L−1/ν (continuous) or L−d (first order) (Janke–Kenna): at one control value they decide order, not LINE-ness.
