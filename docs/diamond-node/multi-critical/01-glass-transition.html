Glass Transition

Two transitions, one bicritical point

A glass is a liquid that forgot to crystallize. Cool a liquid fast enough, and it never finds its crystal. Instead, it thickens, slows, and eventually stops flowing — trapped in a disordered state that looks like a solid but has the structure of a liquid. For a century, the nature of this transition has been one of the deepest open problems in condensed matter physics.

The reason it stayed open is that everyone was looking for one transition. There are two.

Two Peaks, Not One

In 2025, Pettini and colleagues published a landmark study that resolved the glass transition into two distinct events, each with its own thermodynamic signature and its own topological fingerprint in the energy landscape.

The first is a dynamic transition. At a temperature physicists call TMCT (the mode-coupling temperature), the liquid’s molecular rearrangements slow catastrophically. Molecules can still vibrate in their cages, but they can no longer escape them. The system loses ergodicity — its ability to explore all of its possible configurations. This transition is weakly first-order: there is a small but measurable discontinuity, like a tiny jump.

The second is a thermodynamic transition. At a lower temperature called TK (the Kauzmann temperature), the number of distinct configurations available to the glass drops toward zero. The configurational entropy — a measure of how many ways the molecules could rearrange — vanishes. This transition is second-order: continuous, with a diverging correlation length.

Each transition has its own order parameter. Each produces its own peak in the specific heat. Each leaves its own topological mark on the geometry of the energy landscape. They are not two views of one event. They are two events.

Distance as the Meaningful Quantity

Here is where the story turns. Glass-forming liquids come in two broad varieties. Strong glass formers — like silica (SiO2) and boron trioxide (B2O3) — have their two transitions close together. Fragile glass formers — like ortho-terphenyl (OTP) and toluene — have them far apart. Physicists have measured this distance for decades. It is called fragility, and it is one of the most useful empirical quantities in glass science.

But what if fragility is not just a useful number? What if the distance between the two transitions is the distance from a bicritical point — a special location in parameter space where the two transitions merge?

At a bicritical point, two independent phase transitions converge. Their order parameters couple. The universality class changes. New physics emerges from the coupling that neither transition produces alone.

The strong-glass limit — where TMCT equals TK — is exactly this point. Strong glass formers are near the bicritical point. Fragile glass formers are far from it. Fragility is distance from bicriticality.

This reframing turns an empirical classification (strong vs. fragile) into a structural prediction: systems near the bicritical point should show coupled critical behavior with a new universality class — and systems far from it should show two independent transitions with their individual universality classes.

A Third Transition Below

Below TK, a third transition appears. Called the Gardner transition, it splits each glass state into a fractal hierarchy of sub-states — marginally stable configurations separated by vanishing barriers. The Gardner transition produces the scaling laws that govern jamming: the physics of tightly packed disordered particles.

This creates a chain that connects the glass transition to an entirely different physical system. The chain runs: glass bicriticality → Gardner transition → jamming scalings → protein core packing. We follow this chain in Tutorial 04.

The glass transition is the simplest system in this series: two transitions, one coupling parameter, one codimension-two point. It introduces the core idea — that the coupling between transitions matters, not just the transitions themselves — in the most stripped-down setting possible. In the next tutorial, we add a third transition.

◆ Technical Detail: The Two Order Parameters

Dynamic transition (TMCT): The order parameter is the Edwards-Anderson overlap qEA, defined as the long-time limit of the density autocorrelation function. Below TMCT, qEA > 0: the system remembers its configuration. Above, qEA = 0: full ergodicity. In mean-field theory (infinite dimensions), this transition is sharp. In finite dimensions, it becomes a crossover, but the underlying singularity shapes the dynamics.

Thermodynamic transition (TK): The order parameter is the configurational entropy Sc, measuring the logarithm of the number of distinct amorphous packings. In the Random First-Order Transition (RFOT) framework, Sc → 0 at TK: the system runs out of configurations. This is a genuine second-order transition with a diverging point-to-set correlation length.

Pettini et al. detected both transitions through independent topological signatures in the energy landscape geometry: inflection in the mean curvature of energy level sets at TMCT, and a peak in scalar curvature variance at TK. The transitions leave distinct geometric fingerprints, confirming they are independent events with different topological character.

◆ Technical Detail: Fragility as Distance from Bicriticality

The TMCT / TK ratio correlates with the Angell fragility index m. For strong glass formers (SiO2): TMCT / TK ≈ 1.1–1.2, m ≈ 20. For fragile glass formers (OTP): TMCT / TK ≈ 1.5–2.0, m ≈ 80–200.

In the bicritical framework, the ratio TMCT / TK − 1 measures distance from the codimension-two point. A Ginzburg-Landau analysis with two coupled order parameters (qEA and Sc) predicts O(2)/XY universality at the bicritical point — the same symmetry class as the Kosterlitz-Thouless transition. If confirmed, the bicritical point is where topological defect physics enters the glass problem.

Preliminary correlation: r = 0.56, p = 0.09 (N = 10 glass formers). Suggestive but not significant. Needs careful TMCT / TK compilation for 20+ systems with well-characterized fragility values.

◆ Technical Detail: The Gardner Connection

The Gardner transition occurs within the glass phase, below TK. Predicted by mean-field theory for all glass formers, it marks the onset of marginal stability — each metastable glass basin shatters into a fractal hierarchy of sub-basins. The signature: full replica symmetry breaking of the Parisi type.

In October 2024, new results (arXiv 2410.23797) demonstrated that the Gardner transition produces the same scaling exponents that govern jamming — the physics of maximally dense random packings. The connection is: glass → Gardner → jamming scalings → protein packing fraction.

If glass bicriticality is real, then strong glass formers are where the Gardner transition should be most clearly resolved (closest to bicritical = strongest coupling). This is the opposite of current research focus, which studies Gardner in fragile hard-sphere-like systems.

Falsification: Prediction #2 — Glass Bicriticality

Claim: The two transitions identified by Pettini et al. interact at a codimension-two point in parameter space, making the glass transition a bicritical system. The universality class at the bicritical point is O(2)/XY.

Status: Theoretical framework complete. Five-point bicriticality framework applied. Ginzburg-Landau free energy with two coupled order parameters constructed. Predicts crossover exponent φT ≠ 1 at the bicritical point.

Test: Simulate a binary Lennard-Jones mixture at multiple densities. If the two specific heat peaks merge at some critical density ρc with a universality class change, bicriticality is confirmed. If they remain independent at all densities, bicriticality is ruled out.

KILL CRITERIA: If varying density shows the two transitions are always independent — never merging, never interacting — bicriticality is ruled out and Prediction #2 fails.
Falsification: Prediction #9 — Fragility-Exponent Correlation

Claim: Fragility m scales as (TMCT / TK − 1)γ for some exponent γ. If true, fragility is not an independent material property — it is a derived quantity measuring distance from bicriticality.

Status: Preliminary. r = 0.56, p = 0.09 (N = 10 glass formers). Direction is correct (higher ratio = higher fragility) but sample is too small for significance.

Next step: Compile TMCT / TK ratios for 20+ glass formers with well-characterized Angell fragility values. If the correlation strengthens, it supports the bicritical interpretation. If it vanishes, fragility and the TMCT / TK ratio are measuring different things.

KILL CRITERIA: If no significant correlation exists between fragility and TMCT/TK ratio across 20+ systems, Prediction #9 fails.

Tutorial 04: Protein Folding

Connected through the Gardner/jamming chain. The Gardner transition below TK produces the scaling laws that govern jamming — which in turn sets the universal packing fraction (φc ≈ 0.55) found in protein cores. The glass bicritical point sits at the top of a causal chain that ends in the geometry of living molecules.

◆ Detection Question

Two order parameters (qEA and Sc) on the same node set — molecular configurations in phase space. A relational gradient operator extracts differences for each order parameter independently along declared edges.

If the transitions are decoupled, antisymmetric circulation across components should produce zero cross-component contribution — no coupling signal. As the system approaches bicriticality (the strong-glass limit where TMCT / TK → 1), the physical coupling between transitions grows.

The question: Does the cross-component circulation signal grow monotonically as TMCT / TK → 1? If so, it measures what fragility measures — distance from the codimension-two point — but detects it through relational field structure rather than through viscosity curves.
