Detailed Analysis

1. SU(2)k Chern-Simons — The Main Sequence

The most important family. Anyons labeled by spin j = 0, 1/2, 1, ..., k/2. Fusion follows truncated Clebsch-Gordan rules: j1 × j2 = |j1−j2| + ... + min(j1+j2, k−j1−j2). Quantum dimension: dj = sin(π(2j+1)/(k+2)) / sin(π/(k+2)).

k=2 (Ising anyons) — {1, σ, ψ}

Fusion rules:

σ × σ = 1 + ψ
σ × ψ = σ
ψ × ψ = 1

Self-catalysis: σ×σ does NOT produce σ. However, σ×σ→ψ then σ×ψ→σ gives a two-step regeneration cycle. This is catalytic cycling but not direct self-catalysis.

RAF closure: Partial. σ generates {1,ψ} directly but needs ψ as co-reactant to regenerate itself. The full set {1,σ,ψ} is closed under fusion. The RAF condition is technically met (σ catalyzes ψ production, ψ catalyzes σ regeneration), but the catalytic network is a simple 2-cycle, not a complex web.

Universality: No. Ising braiding generates only the Clifford group — not dense in SU(N). Requires magic state distillation for universality.

Dimension growth: dσ = √2 ≈ 1.414. Slowest growth of any non-abelian family.

Too simple. The fusion algebra is a 2-cycle, not a complex network. The failure of braiding universality correlates with the failure of direct self-catalysis.

k=3 (Fibonacci anyons) — {1, τ}

(SO(3)3 subtheory, equivalently the integer-spin sector)

Fusion rules:

τ × τ = 1 + τ

Self-catalysis: Yes. τ produces itself directly.

RAF closure: Yes. All three Kauffman criteria satisfied: (1) Generation — τ generates {1, τ} from a single fusion. (2) Transformation — fusion transforms particle types. (3) Closure — all outputs ∈ {1, τ}.

Universality: Yes. Fibonacci anyon braiding is dense in SU(N) — computationally universal.

Dimension growth: dτ = φ = (1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.618. The golden ratio. Fusion space dimensions follow the Fibonacci sequence: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, ...

The minimal universal model. Simplest possible self-catalytic, RAF-closed, computationally universal fusion algebra. The "sweet spot."

k=4 — {1, j=1/2, j=1, j=3/2, j=2}

Fusion rules for the generating anyon j=1:

1 × 1 = 0 + 1 + 2  (three outcomes)
1 × 1/2 = 1/2 + 3/2
1 × 3/2 = 1/2 + 3/2
1 × 2 = 1
3/2 × 3/2 = 0 + 1
2 × 2 = 0

Self-catalysis: Yes. j=1 fusing with itself produces j=1.

RAF closure: Yes. j=1 generates the full anyon set through iterated fusion. All elements transform others. Closure holds (truncation ensures all outputs are valid anyons).

Universality: Yes (with supplementary abelian anyons for certain gates). The non-abelian sector alone generates a dense subgroup.

Dimension growth: d1 = sin(3π/6)/sin(π/6) = 2.000. Integer quantum dimension. Fusion space grows as 2n.

Self-catalytic with richer fusion (3 outcomes vs 2). Universal. But the integer quantum dimension means less "irrational complexity" in the phase structure. The three-outcome fusion creates a richer catalytic network than τ×τ = 1+τ.

k=5 (SO(3)5 integer-spin sector) — {0, 1, 2}

Fusion rules:

1 × 1 = 0 + 1 + 2
1 × 2 = 1 + 2
2 × 2 = 0 + 1 + 2

Self-catalysis: Yes for BOTH generating anyons. 1×1→1, 2×2→2, 1×2→{1,2}. Every non-trivial anyon produces every other non-trivial anyon.

RAF closure: Yes — strongly. This is a fully connected catalytic network. Every element catalyzes the production of every other element. Maximally autocatalytic RAF set.

Universality: Yes. Braiding is dense in SU(N).

Dimension growth: d1 = 2cos(π/7) + 1 ≈ 2.247. Irrational, like Fibonacci but with a different algebraic number.

Strongest RAF closure of any family examined. Fully connected catalytic web. Multiple generating anyons each self-catalytic. This may be more suitable for complex systems than Fibonacci — the catalytic network is denser. The tradeoff: more particle types means more complex experimental realization.

k=6 and beyond

The pattern continues: more anyon types, more fusion channels, richer catalytic networks. Self-catalysis and RAF closure are generic for k≥3. Universality holds for all k≥3 (with possible supplementation for certain gate sets at specific k values).

Key pattern in SU(2)k:

  • k=2: No self-catalysis, no universality, simple 2-cycle. Below threshold.
  • k=3: Minimal self-catalysis, universal, RAF closed. At threshold (the "Fibonacci edge").
  • k≥4: Rich self-catalysis, universal, strongly RAF closed. Above threshold.

There is a phase transition at k=3 — the onset of both self-catalysis and computational universality simultaneously. This is not coincidental: the same algebraic structure (non-trivial self-coupling in the fusion rules) enables both properties.

2. SU(3)k — Richer Gauge Group

SU(3) theories have more anyon types at each level (labeled by Young tableaux).

SU(3)1

Abelian. Three anyons forming Z3. No interest for complex systems.

SU(3)2

Six anyon types. The fundamental representation has quantum dimension d ≈ 1.932 (irrational, related to tribonacci-like algebraic numbers).

Fusion: f×f = f̅ + (adjoint sector). Self-catalysis depends on whether the adjoint includes f. In SU(3)2, the truncation makes this nontrivial: f×f̅ = 1 + adj, and adj×f = f + f̅ + .... The catalytic network is richer than SU(2) at comparable levels because of additional structure from the larger gauge group.

Likely self-catalytic and RAF closed, with richer catalytic networks than SU(2) at comparable k. Braiding likely universal. The tradeoff: more particle types makes physical realization harder. Confidence in the specific fusion rules is lower here — SU(3) Chern-Simons theory is well-defined but less commonly tabulated in full detail than SU(2).

3. SO(3)k / Metaplectic Anyons

SO(3)k has only integer-spin anyons (j=0,1,...,k). This is not the same as restricting SU(2)2k to integer spins (the relationship involves different modular data).

SO(3)2 (Metaplectic): {1, s, φ}

φ × φ = 1 + s
φ × s = φ
s × s = 1

Structurally identical to Ising (same fusion rules with relabeling σ→φ, ψ→s).

Self-catalysis: No (same as Ising — 2-step cycle only). RAF closure: Partial. Universality: No.

SO(3)k for k≥3

Recovers the integer-spin sector patterns from SU(2)2k. At k=3, the SO(3)3 theory gives the Fibonacci anyon model {1,τ}. The "good" properties emerge here too at k≥3.

The metaplectic label specifically refers to SO(3)2, which shares Ising's limitations. The SO(3)k family as a whole follows the same phase transition at k=3.

4. D(S3) — Quantum Double of S3

The simplest non-abelian finite group quantum double. 8 anyon types corresponding to irreps of the Drinfeld double of the symmetric group S3. Non-abelian anyons include a 2-dimensional charge sector (eC, associated with the conjugacy class of transpositions).

Fusion rules for eC:

eC × eC = 1 + eC + eC′

Self-catalysis: Yes. eC × eC produces eC.

RAF closure: Yes. The generating anyon produces multiple types including itself.

Universality: No. D(S3) braiding is NOT universal — it generates only a finite group of gates. This is a general property of quantum doubles of finite groups: the braiding is too "rigid."

Dimension growth: deC = 2. Integer quantum dimension. Growth is 2n.

Self-catalytic and RAF-closed, but NOT computationally universal. This breaks the apparent correlation between RAF closure and universality seen in SU(2)k. However, D(S3) is universal for classical computation (via non-abelian group multiplication) — just not for quantum computation. For the complex-systems question, classical universality might suffice.

5. Haagerup Fusion Category

The Haagerup category is exotic — it does not arise from any Chern-Simons theory or quantum group construction. Discovered as a subfactor, it has 6 simple objects.

The generating object α has quantum dimension dα = (5+√13)/2 ≈ 4.303 — the largest quantum dimension of any known "small" MTC.

Fusion rules (partial; full rules involve 6 objects):

α × α = 1 + α + α* + β + β* + γ  (produces essentially everything)
α × α* = 1 + α + α* + ...

Self-catalysis: Yes, emphatically. α×α produces α.

RAF closure: Yes, strongly. A single fusion event generates most of the anyon set.

Universality: Open question. Whether Haagerup braiding is computationally universal is not definitively settled in the literature. The large quantum dimension suggests rich braiding, but universality proofs require detailed analysis of the braid representations.

Dimension growth: dα ≈ 4.303. Extremely fast — fusion space grows as ~4.3n.

Maximally self-catalytic (one fusion produces nearly everything), but the fusion algebra may be too rich — like a chemical system where every reaction produces every product. For complex systems, you need structure in the catalytic network, not just maximal connectivity. A fully connected network is actually less interesting than one with intermediate connectivity (same principle as K≈2 vs K=N in Boolean networks).

Patterns

  1. Self-catalysis is generic, not special. Every non-abelian anyon family with k≥3 (or equivalent complexity threshold) exhibits self-catalysis. The only exception is the Ising/metaplectic family (k=2), which has a 2-step catalytic cycle instead. Implication for the AC bridge: the structural identity between anyon fusion and RAF closure is not unique to Fibonacci anyons. It is a generic property of sufficiently complex fusion algebras.
  2. There is a phase transition at the onset of self-catalysis. In SU(2)k: k=2 has no self-catalysis and no universality; k=3 has both. The transition is sharp. The same algebraic structure (non-trivial self-coupling Njjj ≥ 1) enables both self-catalysis and computational universality simultaneously. This is the key finding: the two properties share a common algebraic origin.
  3. The Fibonacci model is the critical case, not the typical case. Fibonacci sits exactly at the phase transition: minimal self-catalysis (one outcome: τ×τ→τ plus vacuum), minimal universality (barely dense in SU(2)), golden ratio dimension growth (the "slowest irrational"). Analogous to K=2 in Boolean networks — the critical point between order and chaos. The Fibonacci model is the edge of chaos for anyon fusion algebras.
  4. Universality does not perfectly correlate with RAF closure. D(S3) is self-catalytic and RAF-closed but not computationally universal (for quantum computation). The correlation holds within SU(2)k but breaks for other families. However, D(S3) is universal for classical computation. If the complex-systems hypothesis requires only classical PCE, the correlation is restored.
  5. There is a "sweet spot" in fusion algebra complexity.

  6. The golden ratio is not unique, but it is special. φ is the smallest quadratic irrational with a self-similar continued fraction expansion [1;1,1,1,...]. This connects to the Fibonacci sequence, which is itself the simplest non-trivial recursive sequence. The "minimal complexity" of φ mirrors the "minimal self-catalysis" of the Fibonacci anyon model. Other growth rates (SU(2)5: cos(π/7)+1, Haagerup: (5+√13)/2) involve different algebraic numbers. Whether these differences matter for complex system dynamics is an open question.

Conclusions for the AC Bridge

  1. The AC bridge via RAF closure is generic across anyon families with k≥3. It is not a special property of Fibonacci anyons. Any sufficiently complex fusion algebra satisfies autocatalytic closure.
  2. Fibonacci is the critical/minimal case — the phase transition between algebras that do and don't support complex systems. This makes it the natural starting point for investigation, but not the only target.
  3. The k=4 and k=5 models may be better targets for finding complex dynamics in lattice simulations, because their richer fusion rules (3+ outcomes) create denser catalytic networks with more room for structured heterogeneity.
  4. The onset of self-catalysis coincides with the onset of computational universality. This is the strongest evidence that the AC bridge is real: the same algebraic structure enables both the topological property (universal computation) and the autocatalytic property (self-sustaining closure). They share a common algebraic root.
  5. Open questions: Does the lattice braiding search find Class IV behavior more easily at k=4–5 than k=3? Is the "sweet spot" at k=3 (edge of chaos) or k=4–5 (richly complex)? Does the Haagerup category produce complex dynamics despite its non-standard origin? Is classical computational equivalence sufficient for the bridge, or is quantum universality required?
