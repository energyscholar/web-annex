More data than unknowns, and still not enough

The companion page, Reading the Sky from the Ground, asked what one ground array sees. This page asks the next question. Several instruments look at the same ionosphere: magnetometers on the ground, a magnetometer on a satellite a few hundred kilometres above the current, a radar that measures plasma velocity along its beams. Each samples the sky differently, each has its own noise, and none of them sees everything. How are they combined into one estimate of the current, and how does one say, with numbers, what that estimate can and cannot resolve?

The answer is linear inverse theory, and its central fact is easy to state and easy to forget. A problem can be overdetermined, with many more measurements than unknowns, and still be ill-posed: some combinations of the unknowns change the data so little that the noise hides them, and some change the data not at all. Adding more of the same measurement does not fix that. Adding a measurement that looks from a different direction can.

The ellipse is the 1σ posterior region for σ = 1 noise. Its semi-axes are 1/si, the reciprocal singular values of G, along the right singular vectors. Doubling the number of near-parallel measurements shrinks it only by √2; widening the spread shrinks the long axis by orders of magnitude.

The forward model: d = G m

Every instrument on this page is linear in the current. Write the unknown current as a vector m of amplitudes in some basis, the measurements as a vector d, and each measurement is a fixed linear combination of the amplitudes: d = Gm + e, with e the noise. Building G is physics; everything after it is linear algebra.

2.1A meridian slab

To see the instruments side by side, take the simplest geometry: currents that do not vary in the east–west direction, a thin sheet at height h = 110 km, and a meridian along which ground stations, a satellite track and a radar beam lie. The sheet is cut into 30 ribbons, 40 km wide, between 600 km south and 600 km north of the array centre. Each ribbon carries two unknowns: an eastward surface current density Ky (this one is divergence-free in this geometry) and a northward one Kx, which can only be steady if it is fed and drained by field-aligned currents (FACs) at the ribbon edges: it is the curl-free part. Coordinates are x north, y east, z down.

Ground and satellite, from Ky. By Biot–Savart, an eastward line current I at height h produces at a point a vertical distance ζ below it and a horizontal distance u north of it

Bx = (μ0I / 2π) ζ / (u2 + ζ2),    Bz = −(μ0I / 2π) u / (u2 + ζ2)(2.1)

Integrating over a ribbon of width x2 − x1 carrying Ky gives a closed form: Bx = (μ0Ky/2π)[arctan(u1/ζ) − arctan(u2/ζ)] and Bz = −(μ0Ky/4π) ln[(u12+ζ2)/(u22+ζ2)], with ui = x − xi. A ground station has ζ = h. A satellite at altitude 450 km has ζ = −340 km: the same formula with the sign of ζ reversed. So the horizontal component Bx reverses across the sheet, exactly so at equal distance above and below an infinite planar sheet, while the vertical component Bz does not, and at a real orbit, three times farther from the sheet than the ground, the geometry factors differ: the satellite sees a smoother, weaker version.

Satellite, from Kx. The northward current and its FACs form, in this geometry, a current system whose field is zero below the sheet (the two-dimensional form of Fukushima’s theorem, tab 3) and, above it, purely eastward and local: Ampère’s law across the sheet gives

By(above) = μ0 Kx(x),    By(below) = 0(2.2)

This is the slab form of the standard single-satellite estimate of FACs from the east–west magnetic perturbation along the track.[17] The ground has a whole column of zeros in G for every Kx: it is blind to it.

Radar, from both. A radar measures the line-of-sight component of the plasma drift v. In the F region the plasma drifts with v = E×B/B2, so E = −v×B (the ideal-MHD relation). The current is tied to E through the height-integrated conductivity tensor,

K = ΣP E + ΣH b̂ × E(2.3)

with ΣP, ΣH the Pedersen and Hall conductances and b̂ the unit vector along B (down, in the north).[16] Inverting (2.3) gives E from K, hence v, hence the line-of-sight velocity: a radar row of G touches both Kx and Ky, but only through an assumed conductance. Here ΣP = 6 S, ΣH = 12 S, |B| = 50 000 nT, and the beam points 40° east of north. The radar is a schematic instrument: no radar data appear on this page.

Spherical elementary current systems

On a sphere, any horizontal current field can be split into a divergence-free (DF) part and a curl-free (CF) part (the Helmholtz decomposition on the sphere). Amm built a basis for each part out of one elementary system per grid point, with closed-form magnetic fields. These spherical elementary current systems, SECS, are the standard way to turn scattered ground and satellite magnetometer data into a map of ionospheric current.[1][2][21]

3.1The fields

For a DF system of amplitude I0 at radius R (= 6371 + 110 km), the current density is J = (I0/4πR) cot(θ/2) êφ, with θ the angular distance from the pole. Its field at radius r < R, with s = r/R, is[2][21]

Br = (μ0I0/4πr) [ 1/√(1 − 2s cosθ + s2) − 1 ](3.1) Bθ = −(μ0I0/4πr sinθ) [ (s − cosθ)/√(1 − 2s cosθ + s2) + cosθ ](3.2)

and above the sheet (r > R, s = R/r) Br = (μ0I0/4πr)[s/√(…) − s], Bθ = −(μ0I0/4πr sinθ)[(1 − s cosθ)/√(…) − 1]. The page uses exactly these expressions; it also checks them, once, against a brute-force Biot–Savart integration of the current density (the readout below the interactive says how well they agree).

For the CF system with its radial FACs, the result is simpler still and follows from Ampère’s law on a circle of constant θ. Below the ionosphere the circle bounds a cap through which no current flows, so B = 0 exactly. Above it, the cap is crossed by the incoming pole current and by a fraction (1 − cosθ)/2 of the outgoing distributed FAC, which gives a purely toroidal (horizontal, east–west around the pole) field[1]

Bφ = −(μ0I0/4πr) cot(θ/2)  (r > R),    B = 0  (r < R)(3.3)

The invisibility belongs to the whole system: horizontal CF current and its FACs. A horizontal CF current without its FACs is not invisible from the ground. It is not a physical steady current on its own (charge would pile up at the pole), but as a mathematical source it produces a ground field of the same order as the DF system; the toggle in the interactive shows it.

3.2Why the ground sees the DF part

Equation (3.3) is Fukushima’s theorem in SECS form:[9] with radial field lines, the ground is exactly blind to the CF part with its FACs, and every ground datum is a function of the DF amplitudes alone. With real, inclined field lines (about 12° from vertical over Fennoscandia) the cancellation is no longer exact and the ground sees mainly, not only, the DF part; see tab 2 of Reading the Sky from the Ground. Two further cautions. First, the DF part is not the same thing as the Hall current: they coincide only if the conductance is uniform. With conductance gradients, Hall currents acquire a divergence and Pedersen currents a curl, and the DF part that the ground sees is a mixture.[20][21] Second, the “equivalent current” mapped from ground data is the DF part of the real current, and nothing more. The checkbox below removes the FACs. The horizontal CF current alone then has a field at the ground, and in closed form: the outgoing FAC spread over the sphere is spherically symmetric and has no field, so the horizontal part is (3.3) minus the field of the incoming line current along the pole axis from R to infinity, Bφline = −(μ0I0/4πρ) [1 − (R − z)/√(ρ2 + (R − z)2)] with ρ = r sinθ and z = r cosθ. A horizontal CF current without its FACs is not invisible.

Least squares, the SVD and priors

Divide each row of G and d by that datum’s standard deviation (from here on G and d mean these whitened versions). Least squares minimises ‖Gm − d‖2. The singular value decomposition shows why that alone fails, and what to add.

4.1Normal equations and the SVD

GTG m = GTd     G = U Σ VT = Σi si ui viT     m = Σi (uiTd / si) vi(4.1)

The right singular vectors vi are patterns of current; si is how strongly each pattern shows up in the data. Patterns with si = 0 form the null space: they can be added to any solution without changing the data at all (the CF columns of the ground block in tab 2 are the plainest example). Patterns with small but non-zero si are worse in practice: their coefficient uiTd/si divides the noise, which has unit size in every direction ui, by a small number.[15][3] For a current sheet seen from the ground, the singular values fall roughly exponentially with the spatial frequency of the pattern, because a field continued downward through a height h loses its structure of wavenumber k as e−kh.

4.2Damping and smoothness as priors

Tikhonov regularisation adds a penalty: minimise ‖Gm − d‖2 + λ‖Lm‖2, so

mλ = (GTG + λLTL)−1 GTd(4.2)

With L = I (ridge or damped least squares), the solution is (4.1) with each term multiplied by a filter factor fi = si2/(si2 + λ): patterns the data see well pass, poorly seen ones are suppressed. With L a difference operator, rough patterns are penalised instead of large ones. The Bayesian reading makes the choice concrete:[19] (4.2) is the posterior mean (and maximum) for Gaussian noise of unit variance and a Gaussian prior m ~ N(0, (λLTL)−1). The damping is a statement about how large or how rough you believed the current to be before you looked; its posterior covariance is C = (GTG + λLTL)−1.

4.3Choosing λ: the L-curve and GCV

Plot the model norm ‖Lmλ‖ against the residual norm ‖Gmλ − d‖ on log axes as λ varies: the curve is usually L-shaped, and its corner balances fitting the data against fitting the noise.[11] Generalised cross-validation picks the λ that would best predict a left-out datum, averaged over all data, without actually leaving any out:[10]

GCV(λ) = nd ‖(I − Nλ) d‖2 / [ tr(I − Nλ) ]2,    Nλ = G(GTG + λLTL)−1GT(4.3)

Since the noise here is known (whitened to unit variance), a third rule is available: the discrepancy principle, choose λ so that ‖Gm − d‖2 ≈ nd. None of the three is a law; they usually agree within a factor of a few, and when they do not, that disagreement is itself information.[12]

Things to try. Slide λ down to the left end: the estimate oscillates wildly while the residual barely improves, the classic noise amplification of (4.1). Slide it right: the estimate becomes a smooth, too-small bump and the residual grows past √nd. In the right-hand panel, the data coefficients |uiTd| follow the singular values down and then level off at about 1: that floor is the noise, and the components below it carry no information (the discrete Picard condition[12]). The filter factors switch off close to where the coefficients hit that floor. Switch the prior to smoothness: the narrow westward channel is penalised much more heavily than the broad jet, because it is rough, which is exactly what that prior says you believe.

Resolution and leakage

Any linear estimate is m̂ = G♯d for some matrix G♯, the generalised inverse actually used. Substitute d = Gm + e: m̂ = G♯G m + G♯e. The matrix R = G♯G says exactly how the estimate is built from the truth.

m̂ = R m + G♯e,    R = G♯G,    N = G G♯(5.1)

For the undamped pseudo-inverse G+ = VpΣp−1UpT (keeping the p non-zero singular values), R = VpVpT: a projection, equal to the identity only when the null space is empty. For the damped estimator (4.2), G♯ = (GTG + λLTL)−1GT, and R = (GTG + λLTL)−1GTG is never the identity: damping buys stability by blurring, and R is the receipt.[15][3] N, the data resolution matrix, says how much each datum predicts itself; its trace is the effective number of parameters the data determine.

Two readings of R. A row k is the averaging kernel of the estimate at cell k: m̂k = Σj Rkjmj + noise, a weighted average of the true current over the map. This is Backus and Gilbert’s idea: the data never give you the value at a point, only a known average around it.[4] A column j is the point-spread function: what a unit current at cell j alone becomes in the estimate. The part of the column outside cell j is leakage: true current in one place reported in another. For ridge damping, R is symmetric and the two readings coincide; for a smoothness prior they do not.

The key lesson. An estimate is a weighted average over the true structure, and the weights are known. They are the rows of R, computable before any data arrive, from the instrument geometry, the noise and the prior alone. A map without its resolution is a picture; a map with it is a measurement.

5.1Posterior covariance, and what it does not include

In the Bayesian reading, the posterior covariance C = (GTG + λLTL)−1 gives the uncertainty of each cell, and it grows wherever stations are missing. A frequentist would quote instead the covariance of the estimate under repeated noise, G♯G♯T, which is smaller: it leaves out the bias (R − I)m. The two are related by C = G♯G♯T + (I − R) (λLTL)−1(I − R)T: the second term is the prior’s estimate of what the blurring hides.[19] Neither covariance includes errors in G itself: the thin-sheet height, radial field lines, ignored induction. Those enter as bias, and only the checks of tab 7 can bound them.

Joint inversion: stacking instruments

Joint inversion is, formally, nothing more than stacking: put every instrument’s rows into one G, every datum into one d, and weight each block by its own noise covariance. The substance is in the weights, in the parts of m each instrument reaches, and in the assumptions some instruments bring with them.

[ Cg−½Gg ; Cs−½Gs ; Cr−½Gr ] m = [ Cg−½dg ; Cs−½ds ; Cr−½dr ](6.1)

6.1What each instrument adds

The satellite adds the CF part through By = μ0Kx (2.2), along its track only, and it measures the DF part again from the other side of the sheet, with a different geometric weighting: the altitude separation between ground and satellite is what lets a combined inversion separate currents in the sheet from currents elsewhere, the principle behind ground–satellite SECS and the published frameworks below.[22] The radar adds E, and links E to the current only through (2.3). This is where the conductance assumption enters, and the only place: the magnetometer rows of G contain no conductance at all. If the assumed ΣH is wrong, the radar rows are wrong, and the inversion will fit them by distorting the current.

Two published frameworks do this at scale. AMIE (assimilative mapping of ionospheric electrodynamics) expands the electric potential in basis functions, ties every instrument to it through a conductance model, and solves a Bayesian least-squares problem with a prior covariance.[16] Lompe (local mapping of polar ionospheric electrodynamics) does the same on a regional grid with SECS-like basis functions, combining ground and space magnetometers, radar velocities and other data, with explicit priors and resolution analysis.[14] What follows is the same idea in the slab of tab 2.

The truth is a convection channel: a northward electric field Ex(x) drives Pedersen current Kx = ΣPEx (CF, with a FAC pair at its edges) and Hall current Ky = ΣHEx (DF), with true conductances 6 S and 12 S. Both halves have a smoothness prior.

The resolution matrix R: rows are estimates, columns are true cells; DF top-left, CF bottom-right. A sharp diagonal means a cell is resolved; a blank block means it is not seen at all; colour in the off-diagonal blocks is leakage between DF and CF, which only the radar, through the conductance, can create.

Things to try. Ground alone: the DF half is resolved near the stations, the CF half has R = 0 and its estimate is the prior (zero) with the prior’s uncertainty: the data say nothing. Add the satellite: the CF diagonal lights up along the whole track and the DF band tightens. Then mis-weight the satellite: tell the inversion its noise is five times smaller than it is and watch its χ2 per datum stay near 1 while the ground’s grows: the inversion now fits the satellite’s noise and distorts the current to do it. Finally, use ground and radar only, and move the assumed ΣH away from 12 S: the CF estimate is biased, and nothing in the error bars says so, because the posterior covariance is conditional on G being right.

Checking an inversion

Resolution and covariance are computed from the model. They are only as good as the model. The checks below test the whole chain: they plant a current whose answer is known, invert it exactly as real data would be inverted, and compare. Nothing else says what a method can recover.

7.1Synthetic recovery: a drifting vortex

Plant a Hall-current vortex, a Gaussian blob of DF current, drifting east or west at a chosen speed across the IMAGE array. Forward-model it to the 23 stations with its own fine grid, add noise, invert each minute with the SECS grid of tab 5, and compare the recovered vortex with the planted one: its position, its peak current, and the speed one would infer from the recovered positions.

7.2Residuals, held-out stations and the prior

Three further checks, on the same planted vortex. Residual whiteness: if G and the noise model are right, the whitened residuals (d − Gm̂)/σ should look like independent unit-variance noise: no pattern across stations, χ2 per datum near 1 minus the fraction of the data spent on parameters. Over-damping shows up as smooth, structured residuals; an unmodelled source as residuals concentrated at a few stations. Held-out stations: invert without one station and predict what it should have measured; a model that predicts its held-out data is worth more than one that only fits the data it was given. The leave-one-out error can be computed for every station and every λ without re-inverting, from dk − Gkm̂(−k) = (I − Hkk)−1(dk − Gkm̂), where Hkk is the station’s 3 × 3 block of N.[10] Sensitivity to the prior: change the prior and see what changes; a feature that comes and goes with the prior is a feature of the prior.

Interactive · residuals and hold-out

The honest way to state what a method can recover is a table of such tests: for each planted speed, size and location, how well the method recovered it. Notice in particular what the recovered speed does as you change the damping. With too little damping the estimate fits the noise, small spurious amplitudes scattered over the grid pull the weighted centre toward the middle of the network, and the inferred drift is biased low: for the default vortex, about 0.3 instead of 0.5 km/s at log10 λ = −2, against 0.49 km/s near χ2 ≈ 1. Shrink the vortex to 60 km instead and χ2 climbs above 2 at any damping: a current narrower than the pole spacing cannot be represented, and the misfit says so. That bias is a property of the array and the estimator, not of the sky, and it is only visible because the answer was known.

9.2Glossary

Averaging kernel
A row of the model resolution matrix: the weights with which the true model is averaged into one estimated value.
CF / DF
Curl-free and divergence-free parts of a horizontal current on a sphere (Helmholtz decomposition). The CF part needs field-aligned currents to be steady.
Conductance
Height-integrated conductivity of the ionosphere, in siemens. Pedersen (ΣP, along E) and Hall (ΣH, perpendicular to E and B).
Data resolution matrix
N = GG♯: how each predicted datum is built from the observed data. Its trace is the effective number of parameters.
Discrepancy principle
Choose the damping so that the residual is as large as the known noise, no smaller.
Equivalent current
The DF current in a thin sheet that reproduces the ground magnetic field. Equals the Hall current only for uniform conductance.
FAC
Field-aligned current: current along the magnetic field, connecting the ionosphere to the magnetosphere.
Filter factor
si2/(si2 + λ): how much of each singular component ridge damping lets through.
Fukushima’s theorem
Radial FACs closed by a CF horizontal current produce no magnetic field below the ionosphere (for radial field lines).
GCV
Generalised cross-validation: a rule for choosing the damping that approximates leave-one-out prediction error.
Generalised inverse
Any matrix G♯ with m̂ = G♯d; the pseudo-inverse G+ is one, the damped inverse another.
Ill-posed
A problem whose solution does not depend continuously on the data, or is not unique; discretely, a problem with a null space or very small singular values.
Inverse crime
Testing an inversion with synthetic data made by the same discretised model it inverts with, which hides discretisation error.
L-curve
Log model norm against log residual norm as the damping varies; its corner is a common choice of damping.
Leakage
True structure at one place appearing in the estimate at another: the off-diagonal part of a column of R.
Null space
Model patterns m with Gm = 0: invisible to the data, unrecoverable without a prior.
Picard condition
Data coefficients |uiTd| must fall faster than the singular values for the naive solution to make sense; where they flatten at the noise level, information ends.
Point-spread function
A column of R: the estimate produced by a unit spike of true model.
Posterior covariance
(GTCe−1G + Cm−1)−1: the uncertainty of a Gaussian-prior estimate, conditional on G being right.
Resolution matrix
R = G♯G: the linear map from the true model to the expected estimate.
SECS
Spherical elementary current systems: DF and CF basis currents on a sphere, one per grid pole, with closed-form magnetic fields.
SVD
Singular value decomposition G = UΣVT; columns of V are model patterns, singular values say how strongly the data see them.
Tikhonov regularisation
Adding λ‖Lm‖2 to the misfit; equivalent to a Gaussian prior.
Whitening
Dividing data and rows of G by the noise standard deviation (or by Ce−½) so that all noise has unit variance.
