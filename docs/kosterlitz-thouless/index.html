Kosterlitz–Thouless for the Working Mathematician

A phase transition with no order parameter, no broken symmetry, and no ordered phase — in which the defects turn out to be the physics.

Every phase transition you were taught works the same way: a symmetry breaks, an order parameter turns on, and something diverges as a power law. This one does none of that. There is no order parameter, no symmetry is broken, and a theorem forbids the ordered phase from existing at all. And yet there is a sharp, experimentally confirmed transition, with a parameter-free prediction that was measured and found correct. It won a Nobel prize in 2016.

1. The problem: a theorem says there is nothing to see

Mermin–Wagner–Hohenberg (1966)

A continuous symmetry cannot be spontaneously broken at any temperature T > 0 in d ≤ 2 dimensions with short-range interactions. Long-wavelength fluctuations — Goldstone modes — destroy the order.1

Take the two-dimensional XY modelSpins that are unit vectors confined to a plane, so each is described by one angle θ. The symmetry is a global rotation of every angle — continuous, and therefore covered by Mermin–Wagner.: planar spins on a lattice, each described by one angle, with

H = −J ∑⟨ij⟩ cos(θi − θj)

Mermin–Wagner says there is no ferromagnetic phase at any positive temperature. So the model should be boring — disordered everywhere, no transition. Except that thin superfluid films, thin-film superconductors and two-dimensional magnets all show something sharp happening at a definite temperature.

Both facts are true. Resolving that is the whole subject.

2. Spin waves: the low-temperature phase is critical

At low temperature neighbouring angles differ little, so expand the cosine:

H ≈ (J/2) ∑⟨ij⟩ (θi − θj)² → (J/2) ∫ d²r |∇θ|²

A free Gaussian field. Two things follow immediately, and they point in opposite directions.

First, the mean-square angle fluctuation diverges logarithmically with system size. That is Mermin–Wagner, confirmed: no true long-range order.

Second, compute the correlation function anyway:

⟨s(0)·s(r)⟩ ∼ (a/r)η(T),    η(T) = kBT / 2πJ

That is a power law, not an exponential. Correlations do not survive to infinity, but they do not die off at any finite length either — they decay algebraically, with an exponent that varies continuously with temperature.

The low-temperature phase is neither ordered nor disordered. It is critical at every temperature — an entire phase of scale-invariant states.

In renormalization-group language, this is a line of fixed points, and it is what makes BKT structurally unlike anything in the standard catalogue. There is no tuning to a critical point here; criticality is the generic condition of the whole phase.6

But the spin-wave calculation cannot be the whole story: it assumed θ is a smooth single-valued field. It is not.

3. Vortices — what smoothness forgot

The angle θ lives on a circle, so going once around a closed loop it may return to itself having wound by 2πn for any integer n. That integer cannot change continuously. It is π1(S1) = ℤ, the same invariant that classifies superfluid vortices and cardiac spiral cores.

Figure 1 — the objects the smooth theory cannot see. Walk the dashed loop and add up how far the spin turns. Zero for the spin wave; +2π for the vortex; −2π for the antivortex. The winding number is an integer, so it cannot be removed by any continuous deformation — you can only annihilate a vortex against an antivortex.

4. The two-line argument that gives the transition

This is the heart of the subject and it fits on a napkin. Kosterlitz and Thouless, 1973 — and Berezinskii, independently and first, in 1971.2

Put one vortex in a system of size L with lattice spacing a. Integrating the gradient energy of the winding field over the plane gives a logarithm:

E = πJ ln(L/a) + Ecore

The energy diverges with system size — a single vortex looks forbidden. But count the entropy. The core can sit at any of about (L/a)² places:

S = kB ln(L/a)² = 2kB ln(L/a)

The entropy diverges logarithmically too — with the same functional form. So compare them in the free energy, and the logarithms factor out:

The Kosterlitz–Thouless estimate
F = E − TS = (πJ − 2kBT) ln(L/a)

The sign of the bracket decides everything.

  • kBT < πJ/2 → F > 0: a free vortex costs infinite free energy. Vortices survive only in bound ± pairs, whose energy is finite.
  • kBT > πJ/2 → F < 0: entropy wins, and the system spontaneously proliferates free vortices.
kBTKT = πJ / 2
Figure 2 — energy against entropy, and what it does to a pair. Both terms scale as ln(L/a), so the competition is decided by a pure number rather than by the system size. Raise T and the entropy line pivots upward past the energy line; at that instant bound pairs come apart and the system fills with free vortices.

The Coulomb gas, which is the same problem

Two vortices with winding numbers ni, nj separated by r interact as

Eij = −2πJ ninj ln(rij/a)

which is exactly the two-dimensional Coulomb interaction, with winding number playing the role of electric charge. So the XY model maps onto a neutral two-dimensional Coulomb gas, and the BKT transition becomes something very familiar: a dielectric-to-conductor transition. Below it, charges are bound into dipoles that only polarize; above it, free charges screen. Everything that follows can be read in either language.

5. The Kosterlitz flow

The napkin argument ignores that other pairs polarize and screen the pair you are pulling apart. Doing that properly is a renormalization group problem in two coupling constants:

  • K = J/kBT — the stiffness, how expensive it is to twist the field;
  • y = e−βEcore — the vortex fugacity, how cheap it is to make one.
The Kosterlitz recursion relations
dK−1/dℓ = 4π³y² + O(y4) dy/dℓ = (2 − πK) y + O(y³)

with ℓ = ln b the logarithm of the rescaling factor. Vortices soften the stiffness; the stiffness controls whether vortices multiply.

Now read the second equation. If K > 2/π the bracket is negative and y flows to zero: vortices are irrelevant and are renormalized away. If K < 2/π the bracket is positive and y grows without bound: vortices take over. The whole half-line y = 0, K ≥ 2/π is fixed.

Figure 3 — the flow, and why this transition is unlike the others. There is no isolated critical fixed point. There is a line of them, and the transition happens where that line terminates. A system below TKT does not flow to a critical point — it flows onto the critical line at a renormalized stiffness and stays critical. The hyperbolic geometry near the endpoint is what produces the essential singularity below.

6. Consequences you can measure

An essential singularity, not a power law

Because the flow near the endpoint is marginal rather than hyperbolic in the usual sense, the correlation length does not diverge as a power. Approaching from above, with t = (T − TKT)/TKT:

ξ ∼ exp(b / √t)
There is no ν for BKT

Quoting a correlation-length exponent for this transition is a category error. The divergence is faster than any power law, and the transition is of infinite order — every derivative of the free energy is continuous through it. This is also the single easiest experimental signature to check: plot ln ξ against t−1/2 and look for a straight line, where an ordinary critical point would give a straight line on ln ξ versus ln t.

The universal jump — a prediction with no free parameters

At the transition the renormalized stiffness takes the value KR = 2/π exactly, and then drops discontinuously to zero. For a superfluid film that becomes a statement about the superfluid density with nothing adjustable in it:3

ρs(TKT−) / TKT = 2m²kB / πℏ²

Every quantity on the right is a constant of nature. The film’s thickness, its substrate, its disorder — none of it enters. And at the transition the correlation exponent is pinned: η(TKT) = 1/4, which follows immediately from η = kBT/2πJ evaluated at kBTKT = πJ/2.

Bishop and Reppy measured it in 1978 with helium films on an oscillating substrate, and found the jump where it was predicted.4

Figure 4 — the universal jump. The superfluid density falls smoothly, then vanishes discontinuously — and it does so precisely where it crosses a line fixed entirely by fundamental constants. No fitting parameter anywhere. This is about as sharp as a prediction in condensed matter gets, and it was confirmed.

7. KTHNY — running the machinery twice

Two-dimensional melting applies the same argument to a crystal, where there are two kinds of defect and therefore two transitions.5

Figure 5 — two-step melting. A 2D crystal does not melt in one go. Unbinding dislocations destroys positional order and leaves the hexatic phase, which still remembers bond directions. Unbinding disclinations destroys that too. Both steps are BKT transitions, and the intermediate phase is a genuine prediction of the theory that was subsequently found in colloidal monolayers.

The three conditions, and what breaks if you drop one

BKT needs two dimensions, a continuous symmetry, and therefore a logarithmic interaction between defects — the logarithm is what makes energy and entropy scale identically, which is the whole trick. In three dimensions vortices are loops and the transition becomes ordinary 3D XY. With a discrete symmetry there are no Goldstone modes, order survives, and you get an ordinary critical point. Neither is BKT.

9. Why this transition matters more than its applications

BKT was the first phase transition understood to be topological: driven by a homotopy invariant rather than by a symmetry, with no local order parameter anywhere in the description. That is now a large field — topological insulators, topological order, anyons — and this was the opening move. Thouless, Haldane and Kosterlitz shared the 2016 Nobel Prize for it.8

For anyone studying convergence across domains, though, the structurally interesting feature is a different one:

The low-temperature phase is critical everywhere, not at a tuned point.
Criticality here has codimension zero.

Almost every other route to critical behaviour requires setting a parameter to a precise value, which raises an awkward question whenever criticality is claimed in a natural system: who tuned it? BKT dissolves that question. A system in the low-temperature phase is critical without anything having been arranged — it is the generic condition of an entire region of parameter space. Any system that can fall into that region gets scale invariance for free.

And the theorem that forbids the ordered phase is the same one that creates the critical phase. Mermin–Wagner is usually taught as a no-go result. Here it is constructive: because long-range order is impossible, quasi-long-range order is what you get instead, and quasi-long-range order is critical.

Before borrowing this for a biological system

Quasi-two-dimensional systems with π₁ defects are everywhere in biology — spiral waves in cardiac tissue, pinwheels in visual cortex, ±½ defects in active nematic films. The defect-unbinding picture is suggestive and worth pursuing. But those systems are driven and dissipative, not in thermal equilibrium, and the equilibrium machinery on this page — the universal jump, the Kosterlitz recursion, the free-energy argument itself — assumes a Boltzmann distribution that a driven system does not have. Driven defect proliferation is typically an absorbing-state transition with ordinary power-law exponents instead. The discriminating measurement is cheap: essential scaling ξ ∼ exp(b/√t) means BKT; a power law means it is something else. Measure before assuming.

10. What to remember

  1. Mermin–Wagner forbids long-range order in 2D with continuous symmetry — and there is still a sharp transition.
  2. Spin waves give power-law correlations with a temperature-dependent exponent: an entire critical phase, a line of RG fixed points.
  3. Vortices are the missing degrees of freedom, classified by π₁(S¹) = ℤ.
  4. Energy and entropy of a single vortex both scale as ln(L/a). Their competition gives kBTKT = πJ/2 on one line.
  5. The transition is vortex-pair unbinding — equivalently, a 2D Coulomb gas going from dielectric to conductor.
  6. The Kosterlitz flow has a line of fixed points that terminates; the transition is where it ends.
  7. Infinite order, essential singularity ξ ∼ exp(b/√t). There is no ν.
  8. The stiffness jump at the transition is universal, and η = 1/4 exactly.
  9. Two dimensions, continuous symmetry, logarithmic interaction. Drop one and it is a different transition.
  10. Criticality at codimension zero — which is the only kind a system can find without being tuned.
