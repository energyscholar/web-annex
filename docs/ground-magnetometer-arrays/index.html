What a ground magnetometer records

A magnetometer on the ground measures the vector magnetic field, about 50 000 nT at auroral latitudes, and almost everything interesting is a disturbance of a few to a few hundred nT on top of it. Those disturbances are made by electric currents 100–120 km overhead, by currents far out in the magnetosphere, and by currents they induce in the ground and the sea. An array of such stations is a crude camera for the currents. This page is about what that camera can and cannot see.

Units and scale. 1 nT = 10−9 T. A sheet of current carrying 1 A per km of width overhead produces about 0.63 nT at the ground (tab 2), so a 100-nT disturbance means of order 150 A/km, a few hundred thousand amperes across an electrojet.

Sampling. Modern fluxgates sample at 1 s or faster. Data centres distribute 1-s, 10-s and 1-min values; the standard 1-min value is a filtered mean centred on the minute.[18] The IMAGE network states its timing accuracy as about the sampling interval, 10 s, and notes that averaging differs between stations, so apparent inter-station time differences can exceed 10 s.[10] Remember this number: it sets a floor under every time delay on this page.

Bands. Continuous pulsations are classed by period (Pc1–Pc5, Pc5 = 150–600 s) and irregular ones as Pi1, Pi2.[19] With 1-min data the Nyquist period is 2 min: Pc3 and most Pc4 and Pi2 are either removed by the averaging filter or aliased.

Sampling and aliasing

Averaging over the sampling interval acts as a low-pass filter: it shrinks short-period signals (the gain of a T‑second boxcar at period P is |sinc(T/P)|) before they can alias, which is why 1-min means largely hide Pc3 rather than aliasing it. Point samples with no prefilter alias freely. Real 1-min products use a tapered (Gaussian-type) filter rather than a boxcar.[18]

Hall and Pedersen currents, and why the ground sees the Hall current

The ionosphere is a conductor in a strong magnetic field, which makes its conductivity a tensor. Between about 90 and 150 km electrons gyrate freely while ions are held by collisions with the neutral gas; that difference is what lets a horizontal electric field drive a current perpendicular to itself.

2.1Ohm’s law in a magnetised, collisional plasma

In the frame of the neutral wind, the current perpendicular to the unit field vector b̂ is

j⊥ = σP E⊥ + σH b̂ × E⊥,    j∥ = σ∥ E∥(2.1) σP = (n e / B) [ νiΩi/(νi2+Ωi2) + νeΩe/(νe2+Ωe2) ],   σH = (n e / B) [ Ωe2/(νe2+Ωe2) − Ωi2/(νi2+Ωi2) ](2.2)

with Ω the gyrofrequencies and ν the collision frequencies with neutrals.[33] The Hall term is easy to read physically. Where electrons are magnetised (νe ≪ Ωe) they E×B-drift; where ions are collisional (νi ≫ Ωi) they do not. The electrons alone then carry a current −n e (E×B)/B2: the Hall current flows opposite to the plasma convection. The Pedersen current, along E, peaks higher, where νi ≈ Ωi. The ionosphere is thin compared with the scales of interest, so one integrates over height to get conductances ΣP, ΣH (siemens) and a sheet current J = ΣPE + ΣH b̂×E (A/m).

Conductivity profiles — a schematic model

Model, not data. An α-Chapman E layer (peak 1.5×1011 m−3 at 110 km for overhead Sun, scale height 10 km) plus a small night-time floor; O2+ ions; collision frequencies falling with height on a scale that grows from 6 km at 100 km to about 26 km at 200 km, normalised so that νi = Ωi at 125 km and νe = Ωe at 75 km; B = 50 000 nT. Real profiles need a neutral-atmosphere model and measured densities. The point is the shape: two layers, Hall below Pedersen, and conductances of a few siemens in sunlight, falling sharply at night. Measured sunlit conductances follow ΣH ≈ 1.5 (Sa cos χ)1/2 ≈ 1.7 ΣP, with Sa the 10.7-cm solar flux (Chatanika radar, χ = 45–85°).[35] The readout shows that empirical value beside the model’s.

2.2Fukushima’s theorem: what the ground cannot see

Field-aligned currents (FACs) bring current down from the magnetosphere and it closes through the ionosphere. Fukushima showed that if the field lines are vertical and the ionospheric conductance is uniform, the magnetic field of the FACs and of the Pedersen currents that close them cancels exactly everywhere below the ionosphere.[12] The reason is a symmetry: the Pedersen current of a vertical line current spreads radially, a radially symmetric current plus its vertical feeder has a magnetic field that is purely azimuthal above the sheet and zero below it (Ampère’s law on a circle below the sheet encloses no current). With uniform conductance and an electrostatic field the Hall current is divergence-free, needs no field-aligned feeders, and is therefore the part of the system that produces a field at the ground. The ground therefore sees, to leading order, the Hall current only, and the “equivalent current” one infers from ground data is the Hall current, not the full current system.[43][20][1]

Where it fails. Field lines at 60–70° are inclined by about 10–20° from vertical, and conductances have strong gradients (the terminator, auroral arcs). Both break the cancellation, and part of the FAC/Pedersen system then leaks to the ground. The theorem is the right first approximation, not an identity.

2.3The 90° rotation

Just below an infinite uniform sheet current J (A/m) the field is ΔB = (μ0/2) J × ẑ, with ẑ down. That has two consequences. First, the ground perturbation is the current turned 90°: an eastward current gives a northward (positive X) disturbance. Second, with J = ΣH ẑ × E (northern hemisphere, b̂ = ẑ) the vector identity (ẑ×E)×ẑ = E gives

ΔBh = (μ0 ΣH / 2) Eh    (uniform ΣH, northern hemisphere)(2.3)

so the horizontal ground disturbance points along the ionospheric electric field and at right angles to the plasma flow. For ΣH = 10 S and E = 20 mV/m that is about 126 nT. For waves, the ionosphere also screens small scales: a structure of horizontal wavenumber k at height h reaches the ground attenuated by about e−kh, so anything much smaller than ~100 km is invisible from the ground, and the wave field is rotated by 90° on the way down.[16]

2.4Drag an electrojet over the array

Model the electrojet as a bundle of parallel line currents at height h. For an infinite line carrying current I along l̂, Biot–Savart gives B = (μ0I / 2π) (l̂ × ρ) / ρ2, with ρ the perpendicular vector from the line to the station. Directly under an eastward jet X is positive and largest; Z changes sign beneath it (negative, upward, on the poleward side). The ground is a conductor: a perfectly conducting layer at depth d adds a reversed image current at depth h + 2d, which boosts X and suppresses Z. The physically better “complex image” puts the image at a complex, frequency-dependent depth set by the skin depth (tab 3).[3]

What to take from this. A single station cannot tell an overhead current of one shape from another; a line of stations across the jet sees the X peak and the Z zero-crossing and locates the jet. A moving jet makes every station’s X rise and fall in sequence, which is the cleanest kind of true apparent motion an array records. Now hold on to that picture and ask how many other things produce the same sequence.

The ground is a conductor

Every time-varying field from above induces currents in the Earth and the sea, and their field adds to what a station records. The induced part has its own amplitude and its own phase, which depend on period and on what lies under each station. Two stations under the same, perfectly simultaneous source can therefore record signals that are shifted in time.

3.1Skin depth

In a conductor of resistivity ρ a field varying with angular frequency ω = 2π/T diffuses in and decays over the skin depth

δ = √(2ρ / ωμ0) ≈ 0.503 √(ρ T) km   (ρ in Ωm, T in s)(3.1)

Sea water (ρ ≈ 0.3 Ωm) has a skin depth of about 5 km at a 5-min period, deeper than most of the sea but only just. The Precambrian shield under Fennoscandia is highly resistive near the surface, and the conducting mantle lies hundreds of km down; the contrast between ocean and continent is what produces the coast effect.[37][4][31]

3.2Two stations, one source: the induction phase shift

For a horizontally layered (1-D) Earth and a source field of horizontal wavenumber κ, the whole response is carried by one complex length, Schmucker’s transfer function C(ω), computed by the standard layer recursion from the bottom up:

kn = √(iωμ0/ρn),   CN = 1/kN,   Cn = (1/kn) · (knCn+1 + tanh kndn) / (1 + knCn+1 tanh kndn)(3.2)

For a uniform half-space C = (1−i)δ/2: a length of order the skin depth, with a 45° phase. The ratio of internal to external field at the surface is (1−κC)/(1+κC), so the horizontal field a station records is Bx = 2Bxext/(1+κC) (twice the external field over a perfect conductor, once over an insulator), and the vertical field is |Bz/Bx| = |κC|.[37] Put a different Earth under each of two stations and the same source gives them different amplitudes and different phases.

Coastal vs inland — a two-layer model, labelled as such

The model. Inland: a resistive shield (ρ1 as set, 150 km thick) over a conducting mantle (20 Ωm). Coastal: the same with a sea-water layer (0.25 Ωm, depth as set) on top. Each station is treated as if its own layered Earth extended to infinity, which is the 1-D approximation. A real coastline is a lateral contrast; it channels induced current along the coast and produces the large vertical fields of the coast effect, which a 1-D model cannot produce at all. The numbers here are illustrative; they are not fitted to any station.

The lesson is in the time shift. A few degrees of phase at a 30-min period is a delay of tens of seconds, which is comparable to the timing accuracy of the archive and to the delays a slow-moving structure would produce across a 100-km station spacing. It is fixed by geology, so it is the same every day and in every direction: a static station-pair delay. Any velocity estimate from an array that ignores it inherits it.

3.3Induction arrows and the coast effect

The vertical field is the clearest fingerprint. Writing Bz = A Bx + B By at each period defines the tipper (A, B); its real part drawn as an arrow is the induction arrow. In the Parkinson convention the arrow points toward good conductors: at a coast, toward the sea. Analogue-model and field studies in northern Fennoscandia find the ocean effect dominating the induction vectors near the Arctic coast, with the vertical field rising sharply as the coast is approached, and local anomalies at some inland sites.[45][44] Under the electrojet itself the source is far from uniform and arrows computed from active-time data mix source and induction effects.

Classification. Induction is neither magnetospheric physics nor ionospheric electrodynamics. It is the solid Earth and the sea responding to them. It belongs on any list of things that make a ground array show station-to-station delays, and it never moves.

Standing waves whose phase slides with latitude

Field lines are strings. Disturb one and a shear Alfvén wave runs along it, reflects off the conducting ionosphere at each end, and sets up a standing wave whose period is the field line’s own. Drive the magnetosphere at one frequency and the field line whose eigenfrequency matches responds resonantly. The ground sees an amplitude peak at one latitude and a phase that changes by about 180° across it. Nothing moves, yet a latitude chain sees a perfectly regular delay pattern.

4.1Alfvén waves on a dipole

The shear Alfvén wave travels along B at vA = B/√(μ0ρ), with ρ the plasma mass density. On a dipole field line of equatorial distance L RE,

r = L RE cos2λ,   B = B0(RE/r)3√(1+3 sin2λ),   ds = L RE cosλ √(1+3 sin2λ) dλ,   cos2Λ = 1/L(4.1)

where Λ is the latitude of the field line’s foot and B0 ≈ 31 000 nT. With the ionosphere as a node at both ends, the fundamental toroidal (azimuthally polarised) mode fits half a wavelength on the line, so in the WKB approximation its period is twice the Alfvén travel time:

T1 ≈ 2 ∫ ds / vA(4.2)

The exact eigenperiods solve an ordinary differential equation along the field line;[38][8] the WKB estimate is good to tens of percent for the fundamental and is used on this page only to set the shape of the eigenfrequency–latitude curve. Longer field lines with weaker field mean the eigenfrequency usually falls with latitude, from tens of mHz near L = 3 to 1–3 mHz (Pc5) on auroral field lines.

4.2Field-line resonance

A compressional (fast-mode) disturbance in the magnetosphere, driven for instance by Kelvin–Helmholtz waves on the magnetopause or by cavity and waveguide modes,[24] couples to the shear Alfvén wave where the driving frequency equals the local Alfvén eigenfrequency. Near that latitude the response behaves like a driven, damped oscillator, with the field line’s eigenfrequency playing the role of the spring:[39][7]

ξ(Λ) ∝ 1 / [ ωA2(Λ) − ω2 − iγω ](4.3)

The amplitude peaks at the resonant latitude Λr with a width set by the damping γ (mostly Joule dissipation in the ionosphere, so larger for larger Pedersen conductance). The phase follows the oscillator: in phase with the driver on the side where the line is stiffer (ωA > ω), lagging by 90° at resonance, by 180° on the other side. The sign of the latitude phase gradient is the sign of dωA/dΛ. With the usual eigenfrequency falling poleward, the phase lag grows poleward and the oscillation appears to travel poleward; where a density drop such as the plasmapause makes the eigenfrequency rise with latitude, it appears to travel equatorward.

A field-line resonance under the chain

Model: eq. (4.3) with ωA(Λ) from the WKB integral (4.2) on a dipole, plasma mass density ρ ∝ r−1 along the line, scaled so that the eigenfrequency equals the driving frequency at Λr. That scaling stands in for the plasma mass density, which in the real magnetosphere includes heavy ions; the WKB estimate of the fundamental is too crude to turn it into a density, so none is quoted. The “rises poleward” option mirrors the eigenfrequency profile about Λr. Station latitudes are CGM latitudes used as dipole foot latitudes, and the 90° ionospheric rotation and e−kh screening, which smooth the peak seen on the ground, are left out. The lower image, latitude up and time across, is what a stack plot of a latitude chain looks like: tilted stripes, the classic look of motion.

Reading a chain. An apparent latitudinal “velocity” from an FLR is v = ω / (dφ/dx), which can be anything from a few to hundreds of km/s and depends on the period. Real motion gives a delay that does not depend on period; a resonance gives a phase that is fixed by latitude, so the delay (phase/ω) scales with the period. That, together with an amplitude peak at the place where the phase changes fastest, is the discriminator.

4.3Pc5 phase that runs antisunward with local time

Pc5 waves (2–7 mHz) also have an azimuthal phase pattern eimφ. In 61 events on an east–west chain the phase velocity was about 14 km/s and independent of frequency, the azimuthal wavenumber growing with frequency as m = (1.4 ± 0.4)f + 0.26 (f in mHz), and the sign reversed abruptly 1–2 h before local noon: the phase runs westward in the morning and eastward in the afternoon, away from the pre-noon sector, consistent with a Kelvin–Helmholtz source on the magnetopause flanks.[30] It is not universal: three quiet-time events seen by radars over more than 120° of longitude propagated westward on both sides of noon.[28]

Pc5 phase around the clock

The dial is drawn in magnetic local time, so a pattern fixed relative to the Sun stands still in it while the Earth, and the array, rotate underneath. m from the empirical fit above; ground phase speed at 65° = 2πf RE cos 65°/m. The animation is slowed by a large factor.

Two things follow for an array. At 10–15 km/s a Pc5 crest crosses a 500-km east–west baseline in about 40 s, below one 1-min sample: the delay exists only as a cross-spectral phase. And the direction flips with local time, so any average over a day mixes opposite signs.

Look-alikes of motion

An array estimates a velocity from delays between stations. Below are eleven well-documented ways to get delays. Some involve something that really moves; many do not; several move but are not the thing one wanted to find. Each is tagged by the physics it belongs to.

MHDmagnetospheric waves and current systems ionosphericionospheric electrodynamics (conductance, Hall and Pedersen currents) atmosphericneutral-atmosphere waves inductionsolid Earth and sea instrumentalmeasurement and processing

Sq and the Earth-rotation sweep

ionospheric

Tidal winds drive a dynamo in the sunlit E region; its current system is two large vortices fixed relative to the Sun, so each station records a daily variation that changes noticeably from day to day.[46] In the ground frame a Sun-fixed pattern sweeps westward at 464 cos φ m/s: 246 m/s at 58°, 196 at 65°, 143 at 72°. Any slow structure tied to local time does the same. At auroral latitudes Sq is weak and entangled with other current systems.[46]

apparent: W, 0.14–0.25 km/s, hours · nothing moves relative to the Sun

The solar terminator

ionospheric

Solar EUV conductance switches on along the terminator, measured as ΣH ≈ 1.5 (Sa cos χ)1/2.[35] Under a steady electric field the Hall current, and so the ground field, changes as the conductance does, station by station in crossing order. The terminator line is tilted by the season, so the crossing order is oblique: a season-dependent apparent heading at Earth-rotation speed. Try it in the interactive below.

apparent: westward and oblique, ~0.2 km/s, hours

DP2 convection fluctuations

ionosphericdriven by MHD

Quasi-periodic fluctuations of magnetospheric convection, coherent with the interplanetary magnetic field, drive a two-cell Hall current pattern that varies almost simultaneously from the auroral zone to the dip equator.[29] The large-scale convection itself is well catalogued.[14] The array sees a common, near-simultaneous signal whose sign can reverse across a cell boundary: after removing the array mean, the reversal is all that is left, and it can look like a pattern with a direction.

apparent: near-zero lag; a static pattern after common-mode removal

Electrojet-boundary migration

ionospheric

The auroral oval and its electrojets move in latitude with activity: auroral boundaries expand equatorward as the Kp index rises.[6] A jet edge sliding across a latitude chain makes X rise or fall in latitude order (compare the electrojet interactive in tab 2). Something genuinely moves, but it is a boundary of a large system, not a localised object, and it moves only in latitude.

apparent: N or S, monotone with latitude, slow

Travelling ionospheric disturbances

atmospheric

Atmospheric gravity waves[15] launched by auroral heating or from below move the ionospheric plasma and modulate conductance.[17] Medium-scale TIDs have periods of 15–60 min and speeds of 100–250 m/s and are seen almost all the time; large-scale TIDs exceed 400 m/s with wavelengths over 1000 km and are mostly, not only, a disturbed-time phenomenon.[22] These are real moving waves, but in the neutral gas; their magnetic signature is indirect and small.

apparent: often equatorward, 0.1–0.7 km/s, 15 min–hours

Pressure impulses (SI / SC)

MHD

A jump in solar-wind dynamic pressure compresses the magnetosphere; the ground records a sudden impulse, explained as a preliminary and a main impulse with their own current systems.[2] A superposed-epoch study of 2058 events at high latitude finds the preliminary impulse starting 1–2 min before the low-latitude response with a 4–6 min rise, and the main impulse about 2 min after it, rising in 6–11 min, with transient current vortices.[27] The onset is simultaneous across an array; the vortices that follow can drift.

apparent: zero lag at onset, then vortex motion

Travelling convection vortices

MHD

Twin vortices of Hall current, fed by a pair of field-aligned current filaments, travel tailward through the dayside high-latitude ionosphere. The first detailed case, near 08 local time, had filaments about 600 km apart east–west moving westward at about 4 km/s and was linked to a sudden solar-wind change.[11] Ground and satellite cases followed.[13] This is real motion of a localised structure — of a current system, not of plasma.

apparent: antisunward, a few km/s, minutes

Quiet auroral arcs

ionospheric

Quiet, discrete arcs are long in the east–west direction, narrow in latitude, and can persist and drift slowly for tens of minutes; they carry field-aligned currents and raise the local conductance.[21] Under a steady convection field the conductance enhancement adds a Hall current that moves with the arc. The ground signal is weak and smeared by the ionospheric screening of small scales (tab 2), so an array sees a slow latitudinal drift of a broad, low-amplitude feature.

apparent: N or S, slow, weak

Field-line resonances and Pc5

MHD

A standing Alfvén wave with a resonant latitude: amplitude peak, 180° of phase across it, and an apparent latitudinal phase motion whose delay scales with period.[39][7] In longitude, Pc5 phase runs antisunward at ~14 km/s, flipping direction before noon.[30] Explore both in tab 4.

apparent: N/S phase slide; E/W ~14 km/s; minutes

Induction phase shifts

induction

The same external field reaches every station at once, but each station adds the field of currents induced in the ground and sea beneath it, with its own amplitude and phase.[31][45] The result is a fixed delay between station pairs that depends on period but not on time of day or direction of the source’s motion. See the model in tab 3.

apparent: static pair delays, any period · nothing moves

Instrument and processing artefacts

instrumental

Timing accuracy of about the sampling interval, with averaging that differs between stations;[10] 1-min values that are filtered means centred on the minute;[18] baseline jumps; temperature drifts; and short-period signals such as Pi2 (40–150 s)[23] aliased by sampling (tab 1). One station out of step is enough to tilt a fitted plane. A result that does not survive dropping any single station is not a result about the sky.

apparent: anything; typically one station drives it

The E-region terminator is where the solar zenith angle at 110 km altitude reaches 90° + arccos(RE/(RE+110 km)) ≈ 100.6°: the ionosphere is still sunlit when the ground is dark. The common thread. Every entry above produces a coherent delay pattern across an array. Several produce one that a velocity estimator will happily turn into a heading and a speed with a high coherence. An estimate is evidence of motion only after the look-alikes that fit the same pattern have been considered and excluded by something other than the estimate itself.

Array processing I: the delay-and-sum beam

If a pattern translates rigidly across the array with velocity v, every station records the same waveform, shifted by a delay that is linear in its position. Undo the delays for a trial velocity and add the traces: at the right velocity they add in phase. Scanning trial velocities and measuring how well the traces add is beamforming, also called slant-stacking.

6.1Slowness and delays

Write the velocity as a slowness vector s = v̂ / |v| (s/km), pointing along the direction of travel with magnitude 1/speed. A rigidly translating signal xj(t) = f(t − rj·s) reaches station j at position rj with delay

τj = rj · s = (rj · v̂) / |v|(6.1)

The delay-and-sum beam for a trial slowness and its normalised power (semblance) are

b(t; s) = (1/N) Σj xj(t + rj·s),    S(s) = Σt [Σj xj(t + rj·s)]2 / ( N ΣtΣj xj(t + rj·s)2 )(6.2)

S lies between 0 and 1, reaching 1 when all aligned traces are identical. It is the time-domain form of the classical beam power used in array seismology.[36] The array is a spatial filter: its ability to separate slownesses is set by its aperture (largest baseline), its spacing (aliasing), and the sampling interval (the shortest resolvable delay).

6.2Plant a moving disturbance

The interactive builds synthetic 1-min (or 10-s) data on the real station geometry: a Gaussian disturbance of chosen width moving at a chosen heading and speed, passing the array centre at mid-window, with independent noise at each station. Optionally an array-wide common signal is added: the same slow variation at every station, as from a distant large-scale current. It then computes the semblance over headings and speeds and reports the peak.

Beam on a planted disturbance

How to read the map. Heading across (direction of travel, clockwise from north), speed upward on a log scale from 0.05 to 30 km/s; colour is semblance S from 0 (dark) to 1. Ring: the planted velocity. Cross: the peak. The top edge of the map is the infinite-speed limit, where all delays are zero: a signal common to the whole array scores highest there, in every heading.

6.3What to try

  • Speed from aperture and sampling. Set 1-min sampling, a long front, and raise the speed. As the delay across the array (aperture/speed, in the readout) shrinks to a few samples, the peak barely stands above the infinite-speed edge of the map (compare “peak S” with “S at 30 km/s”), the heading wanders and the speed is poorly constrained, landing well off in either direction once noise is added. A fast disturbance is also brief: its duration w/v can fall below one sample, so widen it to keep it sampled. 10-s sampling postpones both limits but does not remove them.
  • A localised blob sees few stations. A narrow blob moving along a track that misses most stations is seen strongly by a handful only; the estimate rests on them and the semblance stays low even without noise. A long front is seen by all.
  • The common signal. Add a common signal comparable to the disturbance. The semblance at infinite speed rises, the peak can jump to the top edge, and when it does not, the speed is pulled upward. Tick “remove array mean” and it disappears — together with part of the disturbance itself when the disturbance is large compared with the array.
  • Svalbard. Adding Bear Island and the four Svalbard stations extends the aperture northward but through a gap; for north–south headings it sharpens the speed estimate, for east–west headings it adds little.

Simplifications. Station positions are on a flat local projection (adequate over 2000 km for this purpose but not for precise work). The planted signal translates rigidly; real structures evolve while they move, which lowers S and is one reason a moderate semblance is not, by itself, evidence of anything. Delays use linear interpolation between samples.

Array processing II: frequency–wavenumber analysis, Bartlett and Capon

The beam works in the time domain on one trial slowness at a time. Fourier-transforming each station first turns every delay into a phase, the whole array into one complex vector per frequency, and beamforming into linear algebra on the cross-spectral matrix. That opens the door to adaptive estimators such as Capon’s minimum-variance beam.

7.1The cross-spectral matrix and the Bartlett beam

At angular frequency ω a delay τ is a phase factor e−iωτ, so a rigid translation with slowness s0 gives station spectra Xj(ω) = F(ω) e−iω rj·s0. Collect them into a vector X, estimate the cross-spectral matrix R = ⟨X XH⟩ by averaging over tapers or segments, and define the steering vector aj(s) = e−iω rj·s (equivalently wavenumber k = ωs). The conventional (Bartlett) and the Capon estimators are

PB(s) = aH R a / N2,     PC(s) = 1 / ( aH (R + εI)−1 a )(7.1)

Bartlett is the frequency-domain beam of tab 6; its resolution is fixed by the array response. Capon designs, for each trial slowness, the weight vector with unit gain in that direction and minimum total output power; the result is the minimum-variance estimate above, which suppresses power leaking in from other slownesses and resolves far better when the signal-to-noise ratio is good.[5] Both were developed and compared on large seismic arrays.[26][25] Capon needs R to be well conditioned; with few averages or after projecting out a subspace it is not, and one adds diagonal loading ε, which trades resolution for robustness and tends to Bartlett as ε grows.

Multitaper spectra in one paragraph. The cross-spectral matrix must be estimated from one finite record. Multiplying the record by K orthogonal tapers (Thomson used the discrete prolate spheroidal sequences, which concentrate as much energy as possible into a chosen bandwidth ±W) gives K nearly independent spectral estimates with controlled leakage; averaging them reduces the variance by about K while the bandwidth 2W sets the resolution, a choice made explicitly rather than by accident of windowing.[42][32] This page uses the closed-form sine tapers, a close relative with minimum local bias.[34]

7.3Why a static pattern can masquerade as a slow mover

A static pattern g(r) q(t) has no delays: every station oscillates in phase or in antiphase. Its wavenumber spectrum is the spatial spectrum of g, centred on k = 0 but broad. While the common mode is present it dominates and the maps peak at the centre (infinite speed). Project the array mean out and the zero-wavenumber part of g is gone; what remains is a north–south antisymmetric pattern, half the array against the other half. The steering vector closest to “north half +, south half −” is a wave with a wavelength of about twice the north–south extent, travelling north or south. The estimator reports it, with a speed set by that wavelength and the analysis period (v ≈ λ/T), not by anything moving. Antiphase between halves is symmetric under reversing the direction, so the map shows two opposite lobes or a ridge through the centre along the gradient; with noise, one wins.

Try: wave amplitude 0, common signal 20, gradient 15, remove common mode on. Then change the period and watch the “speed” change with it, the signature of a pattern rather than a mover.

7.4The array response and aliasing

The response of the array to a single plane wave at s0 is the beam pattern |(1/N) Σj eiω rj·(s − s0)|2, the point-spread function of every map above. Its main lobe width is about 1/(f D) in slowness for aperture D, so long periods give fat lobes. A regular grid of spacing d has exact replicas (grating lobes) every 1/(f d) in slowness: a wave of wavelength shorter than 2d is aliased. Irregular arrays smear the grating lobes into sidelobes.[36]

Sensitivity and nulls: what an array can and cannot see

An estimator always returns an answer. Whether the answer means anything depends on two questions that have to be asked before the data are looked at: could this array, with this sampling and noise, have recovered the thing if it were there? And how often does it report something just as convincing when nothing is there?

8.1Size, speed, aperture

Four conditions bound what an array can resolve as motion. A structure of size w moving at speed v across an array of aperture D, station spacing d, sampling Δt, analysed in windows of length T:

  • Spatial sampling: w ≳ d, or the structure slips between stations; and the ground cannot see structure much below the current height in any case (tab 2).
  • Not common-mode: w ≲ D; a structure much larger than the array changes all stations together and has no measurable delay pattern.
  • Resolvable delays: D/v ≳ Δt (or the timing accuracy, whichever is larger); faster things all look simultaneous. Cross-spectral phase can beat one sample when the signal-to-noise ratio is high, but not the timing accuracy.
  • Fits the window: (D + w)/v ≲ T; slower things never finish crossing.

The visible region

The bounds are order-of-magnitude, not sharp: near each line the estimate degrades gradually. 8.2Planted-signal sweeps: the honest way to state sensitivity

The conditions above are rules of thumb. The real sensitivity of a specific pipeline, with its real geometry, filters and noise, is found by injection and recovery: plant synthetic signals with known heading and speed into data like the real data, run the identical pipeline, and record how often and how accurately it recovers them. The result is a map of where the method works. A null result is meaningful only inside the region where the sweep shows recovery; a positive result is plausible only if similar planted signals come back looking like it.

8.3Nulls: what does “nothing” look like?

The other half is the false-alarm rate. Semblance from pure noise is not zero: with N stations and a finite record the maximum over a grid of trial velocities is biased upward by the search itself. A null distribution answers “how high does S get when there is no coherent motion?” One generic recipe keeps every trace intact and shuffles which station position each trace is assigned to. That preserves each station’s spectrum, amplitude and any common-mode content, and destroys only the geometric consistency of the delays. The observed peak is then ranked against the shuffled peaks. It is a cousin of the surrogate-data tests used for nonlinearity.[41]

Shuffled-array null

p = (1 + number of shuffles with peak ≥ observed)/(1 + shuffles). Try “common signal only”: the observed semblance is high, but so is every shuffle, because a common signal is coherent whatever the geometry, and the test correctly says “nothing geometric here”. With noise only, p is uniform between 0 and 1: about one run in twenty gives p ≤ 0.05 by chance, which is what a 5 % false-alarm rate means. A null tests one specific alternative; choose it to match the claim.

Check the test on data where the answer is known. Before trusting a pipeline on the case one cares about, run it where the answer is known: planted signals (does it find them?), shuffled or time-reversed data (does it stay quiet?), and known natural events with independent confirmation (does it agree?). Each look-alike of tab 5 is also a test case: if the pipeline reports Sq or a terminator crossing as a moving object, it will do the same in the data that matter. Decide the thresholds, the windows and the success criteria first; tune nothing on the case of interest.

Glossary

Alfvén speed
vA = B/√(μ0ρ), the speed of shear waves along the magnetic field; a few hundred to a few thousand km/s in the outer magnetosphere.
Aperture
The largest distance between stations of an array; sets the slowness resolution.
Bartlett beam
The conventional delay-and-sum beam in the frequency domain, aHRa/N2.
Capon beam
Minimum-variance distortionless response: 1/(aHR−1a); adaptive, higher resolution, needs a well-conditioned R.
CGM latitude
Corrected geomagnetic latitude: the field-line-traced magnetic latitude used to organise auroral phenomena.
Common mode
The part of the signal shared by all stations (the array mean); carries no delay information.
Conductance
Height-integrated conductivity, ΣP and ΣH, in siemens; a few S from sunlight, tens of S under strong precipitation.
Cross-spectral matrix
R(ω) = ⟨X XH⟩, the N×N matrix of cross-spectra between stations at one frequency.
Diagonal loading
Adding εI to R before inversion in Capon’s estimator, for robustness.
DP2
A two-cell disturbance current pattern driven by magnetospheric convection, varying with the interplanetary field.
Electrojet
Concentrated east–west Hall current in the auroral oval: eastward in the evening, westward in the morning and during substorms.
Equivalent current
The horizontal sheet current that would produce the observed ground field; ΔB rotated 90° and scaled by 2/μ0.
f-k analysis
Frequency–wavenumber analysis: beam power as a function of frequency and horizontal wavenumber (or slowness).
FAC
Field-aligned current, carried along magnetic field lines between magnetosphere and ionosphere.
FLR
Field-line resonance: a standing shear Alfvén wave excited where the driving frequency equals the field line’s eigenfrequency.
Fukushima’s theorem
Vertical FACs with Pedersen closure in a uniform-conductance ionosphere produce no magnetic field at the ground.
Hall / Pedersen current
Ionospheric currents perpendicular / parallel to the electric field (both perpendicular to B).
Induction arrow
The real part of the tipper drawn as a vector; in the Parkinson convention points toward conductors.
L-shell
The equatorial distance of a dipole field line in Earth radii; foot latitude Λ with cos2Λ = 1/L.
MLT
Magnetic local time: the angle around the magnetic pole measured from the anti-sunward meridian, in hours; noon faces the Sun.
Multitaper
Spectral estimation averaging over several orthogonal tapers (Slepian or sine), reducing variance with controlled leakage.
Null distribution
The distribution of a test statistic when the effect is absent, built here by shuffling station positions.
Pc5
Continuous geomagnetic pulsations with periods 150–600 s (about 1.7–7 mHz).
Semblance
Normalised beam power, between 0 and 1.
SI / SC
Sudden impulse / sudden commencement: the ground response to a jump in solar-wind dynamic pressure.
Skin depth
δ = √(2ρ/ωμ0), the e-folding depth of an oscillating field in a conductor.
Slowness
The vector s = v̂/|v|; delays are r·s. Zero slowness is infinite speed.
Sq
Solar-quiet daily variation, from the E-region wind dynamo; Sun-fixed.
TCV
Travelling convection vortices: pairs of Hall-current vortices moving tailward on the dayside at a few km/s.
TID
Travelling ionospheric disturbance: an ionospheric signature of an atmospheric gravity wave.
Terminator
The day–night boundary; at 110 km altitude it lies where the solar zenith angle is about 100.6°.
