The Poised Antenna

What chlorophyll actually teaches

Poised State seriesShadows of the Poised State · The Poised State and Its Critics · The Poised Antenna

For about a decade, the best-known fact about photosynthesis outside the field was that plants and bacteria use quantum coherence to move energy efficiently. That claim has been corrected by the people who made it. What is left over is less famous and more useful.

Here is the short version, stated once so the rest can be read against it. The light-harvesting antenna works in an intermediate regime. The main energy scales are comparable, none dominant, and the timescales are matched too. It uses dissipation instead of fighting it. The quantum step is real, but it is local and fast; the bath around it and the outcome it feeds are classical. None of that needs long-lived coherence, and none of it is controversial.

Every number and every quotation below comes from a published paper, cited in the references at the end. Where a number is computed rather than quoted, the page says so and gives the formula. Where no source gives a value, the page labels it order of magnitude, uncited.

1. The famous claim

What was measured

In 2007 Engel and colleagues pointed a sequence of 40 fs laser pulses at the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex, a small pigment–protein that carries excitation from the antenna of green sulphur bacteria to their reaction centre. The technique, two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy, records how the response to one pulse depends on what earlier pulses did. The sample was held at 77 K.1

Theory said the electronic coherence that produces oscillations (“quantum beats”) in such spectra should die on the timescale of the first population transfer, which earlier experiments had put at less than 250 fs. The beats did not die:

“Surprisingly, the quantum beating lasts for 660 fs. This observation contrasts with the general assumption that the coherences responsible for such oscillations are destroyed very rapidly, and that population relaxation proceeds with complete destruction of coherence.”Engel et al. 20071

In 2010 the same group reported beating in FMO at 277 K for about 300 fs, roughly four times shorter than at 77 K, and wrote that “this data proves that the wave-like energy transfer process discovered at 77 K is directly relevant to biological function.”2 Collini and colleagues, the same year, reported coherence lasting more than 400 fs at 294 K in the antenna protein PC645 of a marine alga.3

Why it spread

A long-lived oscillation is a measurement. What made it famous was the interpretation attached to it. The 2007 paper proposed that the excitation samples many routes at once and picks the best:

“the system is essentially performing a single quantum computation, sensing many states simultaneously and selecting the correct answer, as indicated by the efficiency of the energy transfer… an operation is analogous to Grover’s algorithm”Engel et al. 20071

A plant doing quantum computing at room temperature is a story that travels. It reached audiences far outside ultrafast spectroscopy, and by the time it got there the qualifiers had dropped off. The original paper carried one: before deciding whether non-local effects are present in FMO, “more detailed analysis is needed.”1

The claim also gathered support it did not have. The 2010 paper said the theory of environment-assisted transport “requires long-lived quantum coherence at room temperature,” citing four theory papers.2 Two of those papers had said something different. Mohseni and colleagues tested the Grover reading directly and found that the overlap with the target state “never exceeds the value of 0.4. We conclude that the unitary Grover search algorithm cannot explain the efficiency of the exciton transfer in FMO complex.”4 Plenio and Huelga found that the noise level that best transmits quantum information is zero, and concluded that entanglement “may not be a universal functional resource.”5

2. The correction

The correction came from several directions over about a decade, and it is now the consensus of the field, including several of the people who did the original work.

Theory: the rates already work without coherence

Wilkins and Dattani (2015) ran numerically exact simulations of the full 24-site FMO trimer at 300 K and compared them with Förster theory, which treats transfer as incoherent hopping. For excitation starting on site 1, the incoherent theory “gives a very good prediction of the transfer rates, steady-state populations, and timescale on which the steady state is reached.” Two quotations carry the result:9

“Whether or not coherence occurs at short timescales, it is quenched by around 400 fs”Wilkins & Dattani 20159
“the energy transfer from the antenna to the reaction centre occurs more than 50 times faster than the fluorescence lifetime of the excitation, whether or not coherence is considered. This means that coherence is not likely to improve the efficiency of the photosynthesis.”Wilkins & Dattani 20159

That second sentence is the whole efficiency argument, and it is arithmetic. The excitation reaches the reaction centre long before it can be lost, so there is little left for any mechanism to improve. The same paper adds an uncomfortable detail for the coherence story: where the incoherent theory misses, it tends to over-predict the rates, “suggesting that in some cases electronic coherence may actually slow down the photosynthetic process.”9

Experiment: the long beats were vibrations

Duan and colleagues (2017) re-measured FMO and found no low-frequency quantum beats with dephasing beyond 60 fs.11 Thyrhaug and colleagues (2018) used a pulse-polarisation sequence that suppresses signals from localised vibrations while letting electronic coherence through, and settled the attribution:

“We show that the long-lived QBs are exclusively vibrational in origin, whereas the dephasing of the electronic coherences is completed within 240 fs even at 77 K.”Thyrhaug et al. 2018 (QBs = quantum beats)12

Note the number. Theory had predicted electronic dephasing in under 250 fs, and 240 fs is what was measured. The 660 fs signal in 2007 was real; it was not electronic. The same paper adds that the laser pulses themselves create the coherence, and that sunlight doing the same is a “speculative assumption”:

“Such short timescales strongly suggest that electronic coherences do not contribute to energy transfer in FMO, even under the speculative assumption that such superposition states can be prepared by energy transfer from the chlorosome under natural conditions.”Thyrhaug et al. 201812

The 2010 algae paper had already flagged the same gap in its own data: “cryptophyte algae are obviously not light-harvesting using sunlight that does not arrive in the form of laser pulses as used in our experiments.”3

The consensus

In 2020 eighteen authors, including people on both sides of the earlier argument, published a joint review after more than two years of discussion.14 Its conclusion is not hedged:

“interexciton coherences are too short lived to have any functional significance in photosynthetic energy transfer. Instead, the observed long-lived coherences originate from impulsively excited vibrations, generally observed in femtosecond spectroscopy.”Cao et al. 202014

Their numbers: dephasing of interexciton coherence is “sub–100-fs” at room temperature, and their own FMO calculation gives about 50 fs at 300 K. Then the point that matters most:

“light harvesting is, to a large extent, ruled by processes on time scales of tens of picoseconds. Thus, the efficiency bottlenecks are not found in the subpicosecond intraprotein relaxation, but rather in the orders-of-magnitude slower processes, such as intercomplex energy transfer and subsequent energy transduction steps in the form of electron transfer at the reaction center.”Cao et al. 202014

Put the two numbers side by side. Coherence lasts under 100 fs at room temperature; efficiency is decided at tens of picoseconds. Ten to a hundred picoseconds is a hundred to a thousand times longer than 100 fs (computed: 10 ps / 100 fs = 102; 100 ps / 100 fs = 103). Even the 660 fs of 2007, had it been electronic, would have been gone long before the step that sets the yield.

The review also cleans up a word. Coherence, it says, “is not at all uniquely quantum but is also a well-known property of classical systems, e.g., for the motion of a pendulum or the propagation of electromagnetic waves.”14 Seeing an oscillation with a stable phase does not, by itself, tell you anything is quantum.

The lesson

Small oscillations in a hard measurement were over-read. The data were good; the attribution was too confident, and the deciding number (under 250 fs) was in the first paper all along. And the claim travelled furthest where it could be checked least: the community outside the specialty amplified a signal the specialists could not reproduce. When a result beats a theoretical prediction, the first question is whether you are measuring the thing the prediction was about.

3. What survives: the poised antenna

Take coherence out of the story and something more durable is left. It was written down before the correction, by four of the field’s principals, and the correction did not touch it.

A design rule: make the energy scales equal

Scholes, Fleming, Olaya-Castro and van Grondelle (2011) wrote a box of design steps for building an antenna. Step D reads:7

“D. Juggle energy scales to produce optimal resonance conditions. This involves considering the following energy scales and setting them to be approximately equal:”Scholes et al. 2011, Box 27
  • D1The electronic coupling between nearest-neighbour chromophores.
  • D2Homogeneous line-broadening (defined by the reorganization energy).
  • D3Site energy differences and/or the grouping of chromophores with similar transition energies.
  • D4Disorder (inhomogeneous broadening).
  • D5The environmental relaxation time — that is, it is not only the fluctuation amplitude caused by the environment that matters, but also the timescales of those fluctuations.

Read D5 twice. The rule is not only about matching energies; it says timescales have to match as well. And the same review says real antennas are built this way:

“photosynthetic light-harvesting complexes generally conform to an intermediate case where the electronic coupling between chromophores is similar in magnitude to coupling to the environment and to the disorder in site energies… Predictive theories are most easily formulated when one energy scale outweighs all others, which is not the case in the intermediate coupling regime.”Scholes et al. 20117

The 2020 consensus says the same thing in its own words:

“The interpigment coupling strength is often on the same order of magnitude as the interaction with the environment (bath), which, in combination with static disorder, results in a tendency to localize the excitation over a small number of pigments even in strongly coupled antenna complexes (e.g., order three to five pigments for LH1 and LH2).”Cao et al. 202014

Here is what that looks like in numbers for FMO. These are the model parameters used in the environment-assisted transport papers, and the thermal energy at room temperature is added for scale:

Bounded above: too much coupling quenches

More is not always better. The same review sets an upper limit on how strongly the pigments can be coupled to each other and to their surroundings:

“a limit to how large the coupling between chromophore transitions and the environment (line broadening) can be, because large interchromophore electronic couplings are usually accompanied by low-lying charge-transfer states and therefore quenching.”Scholes et al. 20117

On the noise axis, the transport theory papers find a window bounded on both sides. With no noise, a detuned site blocks transfer; with some, the energy levels broaden and overlap; with too much, the benefit goes away again:

“with increasing dephasing rate, the broadened lines of neighboring sites begin to overlap and the population transfer will be enhanced as resonant modes are now available. Enhancing the dephasing rate further will eventually lead to a weakening of the transfer as the modes are distributed over a very large interval and resonant modes have a small weight.”Plenio & Huelga 20085

Nature exploits dissipation

The 2020 review’s positive conclusion turns the old picture around. The protein is not a shield that keeps the environment out; it is a channel that uses it:

“Nature does not engineer the bath to avoid decoherence to direct functional processes; such an approach almost certainly would not be robust. Nature, rather than trying to avoid dissipation, specifically exploits it together with the engineering of site energies and excitonic coupling to direct energy transport… The basic physics behind thermalization is used to impose direction.”Cao et al. 202014

In plain terms: higher-energy pigments sit toward the outside, lower-energy ones near the reaction centre, and the bath carries off the excess energy at each downhill step. Heat loss is what makes the flow one-way.

Environment-assisted transport, and what it does not compare

The transport theory behind this is called environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT).4,5,6 In one model of FMO it gives three regimes as the noise is turned up:

Rebentrost and colleagues state the condition for the best case: “The maximum efficiency of ENAQT occurs when the decoherence rate is comparable to the energy scales of the coherent system as defined by the energy mismatch between states and the hopping terms.”6 Once again, it is a matched-scales condition.

Read the comparison carefully

Every one of these results compares noisy transport with perfectly coherent transport. None compares quantum transport with classical transport, and none shows a quantum advantage. The experiments make that sharper:

  • The best-case numbers depend on the model. Three treatments of the same complex give 80 → 94%, 70–85 → 99% and 55 → 99%.4,5,6
  • The first direct observation, in a photonic chip, measured (7.6 ± 1.2)%, and there the efficiency “increases monotonically with the strength of decoherence, meaning there is no optimal level of decoherence in this model.”10
  • A ten-ion experiment saw the full window, best around noise equal to the strongest coupling, and found that at that best point “the transport is mainly diffusive, displaying coherences only at very short times.”13 Where the environment helps most, the dynamics is closest to a classical random walk.

What of the oscillations?

The long-lived beats were vibrational, but the vibrations are not irrelevant. Thyrhaug and colleagues also found coherence in mixed electronic–vibrational (vibronic) states, and wrote that “the presence of such states suggests that vibronic coupling is relevant for photosynthetic energy transfer.”12 Chin and colleagues give the condition for vibrations to matter, and it is another matched-scales condition: mode-driven coherence will be prominent “whenever vibrational modes have frequencies comparable to exciton energy differences of strongly coupled chromophores.”8 In their FMO model a 180 cm−1 mode, close to the 150–170 cm−1 exciton splitting, matters; a 37 cm−1 mode does not. The 2020 review calls this vibronic physics “a field of significant current interest” and warns that “considerable care must be taken” before linking it to biological function.14

4. Quantum here, classical there

If there is no long-lived coherence, where is the quantum mechanics? One number answers it: the mean occupation number of an oscillator of frequency ω in equilibrium at temperature T.

n = 1 / (eℏω/kT − 1) Bose–Einstein occupation. When ℏω » kT, n ≈ e−ℏω/kT: the mode sits in its ground state and every excitation is a single quantum event. When ℏω « kT, n ≈ kT/ℏω: the mode holds many quanta and behaves classically.

Three cases, all computed from that formula at 300 K, where kT ≈ 25.85 meV:

  • The electronic excitation. Absorbing a red photon puts about 2 eV into a pigment. Then ℏω/kT ≈ 77, so n ≈ e−77 ≈ 2.5 × 10−34. Thermal energy essentially never does this. Each excitation is one quantum, put in by one photon. Quantum.
  • The protein’s vibrations. The modes that appear in the FMO papers run from about 37 cm−1 to 210 cm−1,8,12 that is about 4.6 to 26 meV (computed: 1 cm−1 = 0.124 meV). That is right around kT itself (≈ 208 cm−1), so n runs from about 5 down to about 0.6. These modes are thermally populated, so they act as a warm, fluctuating bath. Classical-ish.
  • A slow collective mode. Any slow oscillation of a large system, such as a sound wave or a mechanical vibration at 1 kHz, has kT/ℏω ≈ 6 × 109. It holds billions of quanta and obeys classical equations of motion. Classical.

Occupation-number explorer

Slide the temperature down to 77 K and watch the vibrations. At 180 cm−1, n falls from about 0.7 to about 0.04, so the bath gets much quieter. That is part of why coherence lasts longer in the cold experiments than at physiological temperature. The 2 eV excitation hardly changes: it was in its ground state at 300 K and stays there.

The point to land

The quantum step is local and fast: one photon, one excitation, shared across a few pigments for a few tens of femtoseconds. The bath and the outcome are classical: a thermally populated protein takes up the excess energy at each step, and what comes out at the far end is a chemical product counted in molecules. Quantum mechanics is needed to describe the first step. It is not what makes the whole process efficient.

Two things stand out. First, the bath moves faster than the coherence lasts: the protein’s memory of its own fluctuations is under 50 fs,6 and that is why coherence dies within about 100 fs. Second, the stage that sets the yield, light harvesting across complexes at 10–100 ps,7 finishes well before the deadline set by the excited state’s own lifetime, about 1 ns.6,9 That gap is the efficiency. It is the same arithmetic Wilkins and Dattani used: the useful process outruns the loss process.

6. What it does and does not show

It shows

A working natural system that runs in an intermediate regime: its couplings, broadenings, disorder and bath are comparable in energy,7,14 its design rule asks for matched timescales as well,7 and it exploits dissipation to give energy transfer a direction.14

It does not show a quantum efficiency advantage

Transfer to the reaction centre is more than 50 times faster than the excited state’s lifetime “whether or not coherence is considered.”9 Electronic coherence is too short-lived to matter.14 Where environment-assisted transport works best, the transport is “mainly diffusive.”13

It does not show tuning or selection

The intermediate regime is where antennas are found. That alone does not show that evolution selected for it. Scholes and colleagues note that most antennas lack symmetric order and that “very highly optimal configurations can always be found simply by statistical sampling.”7 Mohseni and colleagues found that FMO “shows relatively small susceptibilities, suggesting robustness with respect to external or evolutionary perturbations”:4 it sits on a broad plateau, not a sharp peak.

7. The reading path

Ordered for someone who has read none of these. Each entry says what the paper claims, what to read first, and what later work did to it. Page counts are for the versions cited.

Start here — the correction and the rule

1. The consensus

Cao et al. (2020), “Quantum biology revisited”, Science Advances 6, eaaz488814

Eighteen authors on both sides of the earlier argument conclude that interexciton coherence is too short-lived to matter for energy transfer, and that the long beats were vibrations. Read first: the abstract, the section on time scales (the “tens of picoseconds” passage), and the paragraph on dissipation. What later work did: this is the current position; nothing in this list overturns it. Open access.

2. The design rule

Scholes, Fleming, Olaya-Castro & van Grondelle (2011), “Lessons from nature about solar light harvesting”, Nature Chemistry 3, 763–7747

A review of how antennas are built: concentration without quenching, regulation, photoprotection, and Box 2’s design steps. Read first: Box 2 (step D) and the paragraph on the intermediate coupling regime. What later work did: its sections on long-lived coherence were written before the re-attribution and are superseded; its design-rule content is not. 12 pp.

3. The rate argument

Wilkins & Dattani (2015), “Why Quantum Coherence is Not Important in the Fenna-Matthews-Olsen Complex”, J. Chem. Theory Comput. 11, 3411–34199

Exact simulations of the full FMO trimer at 300 K, compared with incoherent Förster rates. Read first: the abstract and the long-time panels of the HEOM-vs-Förster figures. What later work did: the experimental re-measurements of 2017–2018 reached the same conclusion from the other side. The paper does not model the reaction centre, and says so; its efficiency claim rests on the rate gap. Free preprint, 19 pp.

Then the original claim, and how it was settled

4. The landmark

Engel et al. (2007), “Evidence for wavelike energy transfer through quantum coherence in photosynthetic systems”, Nature 446, 782–7861

660 fs of quantum beating in FMO at 77 K, read as electronic coherence, with the Grover-search analogy. Read first: Figure 2 and the paragraph that gives the 250 fs theoretical expectation. What later work did: the beats were re-attributed to vibrations; the theoretical expectation turned out to be right. 5 pp.

5. The physiological-temperature leg

Panitchayangkoon et al. (2010), “Long-lived quantum coherence in photosynthetic complexes at physiological temperature”, PNAS 107, 12766–127702

Beating in FMO up to 277 K, about 300 fs. Read first: the rephasing/non-rephasing test (Figure 2), a clever control that separates coherence from population decay but not electronic from vibrational coherence. What later work did: the 2020 consensus finds no long-lived interexciton coherence at either cryogenic or physiological temperature. Free preprint.

6. Ambient temperature, and the sunlight caveat

Collini et al. (2010), “Coherently wired light-harvesting in photosynthetic marine algae at ambient temperature”, Nature 463, 644–6473

Coherence over 400 fs at 294 K in an algal antenna protein, with bilins covalently bound to the protein. Read first: the authors’ own caveat that sunlight does not arrive as laser pulses, and their admission that the observable mixes vibrational and electronic parts. What later work did: the laser-preparation objection became central to the correction. 5 pp.

7. The re-attribution

Thyrhaug et al. (2018), “Identification and characterization of diverse coherences in the Fenna–Matthews–Olson complex”, Nature Chemistry 10, 780–78612

Polarisation control suppresses vibrational signals and shows that the long beats are vibrational and that electronic coherence is gone within 240 fs even at 77 K. Read first: the pulse-sequence figure and the oscillation maps. What later work did: it is one of the primary results behind the 2020 consensus; its finding of vibronic coherence is still open. 7 pp.

Environment-assisted transport — read with the caveat in mind

8. The two founding theory papers

Plenio & Huelga (2008), New J. Phys. 10, 1130195; Rebentrost et al. (2009), New J. Phys. 11, 0330036

Noise can help transport by broadening detuned levels into resonance, with a window bounded by too little and too much noise. Read first: Plenio & Huelga’s paragraph on line broadening (and their finding that a uniform chain gains nothing from noise); Rebentrost’s three-regime figure. What later work did: the effect was seen in engineered systems; the magnitudes are model-dependent, and the comparison is always with purely coherent transport. Free preprints, 7 pp each.

9. The Grover test

Mohseni, Rebentrost, Lloyd & Aspuru-Guzik (2008), J. Chem. Phys. 129, 1741064

A more realistic bath treatment of FMO, an efficiency defined independently of downstream chemistry, and a direct test of the Grover reading, which fails. Read first: Section V (the Grover test) and the susceptibility figure. What later work did: the exact calculations of 2015 are the check on its approximations. Free preprint, 9 pp.

10. The two experiments

Biggerstaff et al. (2016), Nature Communications 7, 1128210; Maier et al. (2019), Phys. Rev. Lett. 122, 05050113

A photonic chip shows the effect directly, small and with no optimum; ten trapped ions show the full window and find the best point diffusive. Read first: Biggerstaff’s efficiency-vs-wavelength figure; Maier’s Figure 1 and Figure 2. What later work did: these are engineered analogues, not biology; neither claims photosynthesis works this way. Biggerstaff is open access.

11. Vibrations that matter

Chin et al. (2013), Nature Physics 9, 113–1188

Vibrational modes near-resonant with an exciton splitting can regenerate electronic coherence rather than protect it. Read first: the resonance condition and the authors’ own warning that ensemble measurements could “completely mask” the effect. What later work did: vibronic coupling remains open in the 2020 review, with care urged before linking it to function. Free preprint, 12 pp.
