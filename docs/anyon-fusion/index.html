The simplest model of universal topological quantum computation.

Section 1

Two Particles, One Rule

In the simplest universal model of quantum computation, there are only two kinds of particle. Everything follows from one fusion rule.

These particles are anyons — quasiparticles that exist only in two-dimensional systems and obey exchange statistics that are neither bosonic nor fermionic. Fibonacci anyons are the simplest anyon model that supports universal quantum computation.

Section 2

The Fusion Rules

Anyon fusion is the quantum analog of adding angular momenta. When two anyons meet, their topological charges combine according to algebraic rules — not resonance, not collision, not interference. Algebra.

Rule 1: 1 × 1 = 1

Vacuum fused with vacuum produces vacuum. The trivial case.

Rule 2: 1 × τ = τ

Vacuum acts as the identity. Fusing vacuum with τ leaves τ unchanged.

Rule 3: τ × τ = 1 + τ

This is the rule. Two Fibonacci anyons fuse into a quantum superposition of vacuum and a new Fibonacci anyon. Everything else in this tutorial follows from here.

Definition: Fusion

Fusion is deterministic algebra, not probabilistic collision. The ‘+’ in τ × τ = 1 + τ means the outcome space contains both possibilities — the system is in a quantum superposition of having produced vacuum or a new τ anyon. Which outcome you get is determined only when measured.

Section 3

The Fibonacci Connection

When you fuse n Fibonacci anyons sequentially, the number of valid fusion paths follows the Fibonacci sequence. This is not a metaphor — it is a direct algebraic consequence of the fusion rule.

Consider fusing n anyons of type τ left to right. At each step, the intermediate result can be 1 or τ. The fusion rule determines which branches are valid:

  • If the intermediate charge is 1, fusing with τ gives only τ (one branch).
  • If the intermediate charge is τ, fusing with τ gives 1 + τ (two branches).

Let an = number of paths ending in 1, and bn = number ending in τ. Then:

an+1 = bn    bn+1 = an + bn

This is exactly the Fibonacci recurrence. The total dimension of the fusion space (both charge sectors) is an + bn.

The Fibonacci sequence does not appear here by analogy. The fusion rule τ × τ = 1 + τ generates the recurrence F(n) = F(n−1) + F(n−2) directly. The branching structure of the fusion tree is the Fibonacci recurrence.

Section 4

The Fusion Space

Quantum information in a topological quantum computer is not stored in individual anyons. It is stored in the fusion space — the space of valid fusion paths through the tree.

Definition: Fusion Space

The fusion space is a Hilbert space whose basis states are the valid fusion paths. A system of n Fibonacci anyons with fixed total charge has a fusion space of dimension F(n−1) for total charge 1, or F(n) for total charge τ, where F is the Fibonacci sequence (with F(1) = F(2) = 1).

No measurement on any single anyon reveals which fusion path the system is on. This is topological non-locality — the qubit is encoded in the collective state of all the anyons, inaccessible to any local probe.

Section 5

Braiding as Computation

When you physically exchange two anyons — moving one around the other in the plane — this applies a unitary transformation to the fusion space. Different exchange sequences produce different quantum gates.

In three dimensions, exchanging two identical particles twice returns the system to its original state. In two dimensions, the second exchange is topologically distinct from the first — the worldlines form a braid, and the braid group is infinite and non-abelian.

For three Fibonacci anyons with total charge τ, the fusion space is two-dimensional — a single qubit. The braid generators σ1 and σ2 act as 2×2 unitary matrices whose entries involve e4πi/5 and the golden ratio φ.

Theorem (Freedman, Larsen, Wang 2002)

The image of the braid group representation for Fibonacci anyons is dense in SU(2). That is, any single-qubit unitary transformation can be approximated to arbitrary precision by a finite sequence of braids. Combined with the ability to perform entangling gates between qubit pairs, braiding Fibonacci anyons is sufficient for universal quantum computation.

For Fibonacci anyons, braiding alone is sufficient for universal quantum computation. Any quantum algorithm can be compiled into a sequence of anyon exchanges. No additional error correction is needed — the topological nature of the encoding provides it intrinsically.

Section 6

Topological Protection

The central promise of topological quantum computation is fault tolerance by physics. The quantum gates are determined by the topology of the anyon worldlines — which strands cross over which — not by the precise geometric path taken.

Continuous deformations of the anyon paths — noise, vibration, imprecise control — do not change the topology. The gate is the same whether the path is smooth or jagged, fast or slow, as long as the same strands cross in the same order.

The only errors that matter are discrete topological errors: an overcrossing mistakenly performed as an undercrossing, or a braid applied to the wrong pair of anyons. These are qualitatively different from the continuous noise that plagues conventional quantum computers.

Topological computation is immune to continuous perturbations. Only discrete topological errors — wrong crossings — can cause mistakes. This is why topological quantum computing is inherently fault-tolerant: the physics provides error correction that conventional approaches must engineer.

The Autocatalytic Connection

The Fibonacci anyon fusion algebra satisfies a structural pattern that appears throughout self-sustaining systems — Kauffman's three criteria for autocatalytic closure.

Generation

τ × τ → τ

The system produces its own particle type from fusion. No external factory needed. Self-generating.

Transformation

The fusion space states are transformed by the system's own dynamics — particle exchange. Self-transforming.

Closure

Every fusion outcome is already a member of the model. Nothing escapes the algebra. Self-contained.

Section 8

Where to Find Them

Fibonacci anyons are predicted to exist as quasiparticle excitations in certain fractional quantum Hall states — specifically the ν = 12/5 state, as described by the Read–Rezayi model (1999). They have not yet been experimentally confirmed.

This is not a materials problem. The fractional quantum Hall effect occurs in GaAs heterostructures, graphene, ZnO, and Si/SiGe — the substrate does not define the phenomenon. What matters are the conditions: low temperature, strong magnetic field, high-mobility two-dimensional electron gas. The same topological order can emerge in any material that provides the right conditions. (Anchor 1: substrate independence.)

Anyons are collective excitations — they are generated by the substrate, not imported into it. The two-dimensional electron system, under the right conditions, spontaneously organizes into a topologically ordered phase whose elementary excitations carry fractional charge and non-abelian braiding statistics. The substrate does not merely host the anyons; it generates them. (Anchor 2: substrate generativity.)

The search continues. Recent experiments on the ν = 5/2 state (which may host a simpler non-abelian anyon, the Ising anyon) have shown promising signatures. The ν = 12/5 state, where Fibonacci anyons are expected, is more fragile and less studied — but the algebraic structure described in this tutorial is waiting to be found there.
